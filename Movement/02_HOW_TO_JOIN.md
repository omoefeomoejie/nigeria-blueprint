# The Membership Standard

*How a person becomes a member, and why it works this way.*

---

## Why there is an exam at all

Three reasons, and none of them is exclusivity for its own sake.

**Because a movement whose members cannot explain its evidence becomes a
crowd.** A crowd repeats slogans it cannot defend, loses the first serious
argument it meets, and can be bought, split, or redirected by whoever pays
last.

**Because we are asking Nigeria to be governed by demonstrated competence,
and we cannot ask for what we will not do.** A platform that demands merit
selection in the civil service (Chapter 2) while admitting anyone who turns
up has already lost the argument.

**Because it changes what membership means.** Everywhere else, a party
member is a person who registered. Here, a member is a person who can prove
they understand the programme. That is a different asset, and it is the one
thing money cannot manufacture quickly.

---

## Why it is NOT the CCP model — and this is important

The obvious comparison is the Chinese Communist Party, which also examines
its members. We reject the model deliberately, and the reason comes from our
own evidence.

**The CCP sets its own exam, marks its own exam, promotes its own passers,
and audits itself.** That is the purest example in the world of an
organisation that keeps its own score.

Our scorekeeper finding says exactly what that produces: **where the body
being measured controls the measurement, you get monopoly, not merit.** It
is the same structure that gave Lagos 375 of 376 council seats, Osun 332 of
332, and Ondo 203 of 203 — three states, two different parties, one
mechanism.

**A platform that sets and marks its own membership test with no external
check has built, in miniature, the exact structure its own Chapter 8 calls
broken.**

So we take the mechanism and reject the model. The mechanism — *prove you
understand this before you speak for us* — is good. The governance comes
from professional bodies, not from a party.

---

## The model we do use: the professional institute

Nigeria already runs credible examination systems, at scale, with published
standards. ICAN examines across three levels with **a published 50% pass
mark**, a published syllabus revised against international standards, and
examination centres in more than thirty cities including Kaduna, Kano, Jos,
Maiduguri, Sokoto, Yola, Port Harcourt and Yenagoa [Certified].

WAEC, NECO and JAMB run standardised examinations nationally, and states
already rent JAMB-accredited CBT centres for their own recruitment testing —
Adamawa used thirteen statewide in 2025 [Certified].

**We do not need to build examination infrastructure. We need to rent it and
publish our standards.**

## The standard

### Structure: three levels, sat in order

**Level 1 — The Findings.** What we established and how confident we are.
Revenue and the three jobs. Absorption. The scorekeeper test. What the
Constitution blocks and what it does not. What things cost.
*Format: 40 multiple-choice and short-answer questions. Pass mark 50%.*

**Level 2 — The Method.** Why we believe what we believe. Evidence statuses.
Why Lagos was demoted. Why we retired Osun's growth claim with our own
arithmetic. Why an interested source cannot certify a mechanism. What
"stock" and "flow" mean and why a reform can be permanent in one and dead in
the other.
*Format: structured questions requiring written answers. Pass mark 50%.*

**Level 3 — The Defence.** One case study and one adversarial question.
Given a real Nigerian policy claim, assign its evidence status and name the
fact that would settle it. Then: *state the strongest argument against this
platform's central finding.* A candidate who cannot argue against us has not
understood us.
*Format: written, externally marked. Pass mark 50%.*

### Governance of the exam — the part that matters most

1. **The syllabus is published in full, in advance.** Anyone may study it
   without joining, paying, or telling us who they are.
2. **The pass mark is 50%, published, and fixed before each diet.** It is
   never adjusted after results are seen. *(Kaduna's 2017 teacher test drew
   an independent audit finding a pass mark far above the West African
   standard and computation errors in the marking. We will not repeat it.)*
3. **Level 3 is marked by an external examiner** who is not a member of this
   platform and is paid whether candidates pass or fail. Without this the
   exam fails our own scorekeeper test.
4. **Pass rates are published every diet**, by level and by state. If our
   pass rate is 12%, that number goes up.
5. **An appeals process exists and is published**, with a different marker.
6. **The exam is free or near-free to sit.** A qualification that only the
   comfortable can attempt reproduces the elite capture we are against. Cost
   recovery comes from elsewhere, never from the gate.
7. **Available in English and Pidgin from the first diet**, and in Hausa,
   Yoruba and Igbo as soon as reviewed translations exist. A test that
   filters by language filters by class.

### Who may sit

Any Nigerian, anywhere, of voting age. No sponsor, no nomination, no fee
barrier, no requirement to be known to anyone already inside.

**Deliberately excluded as criteria:** state of origin, religion, ethnicity,
party history, education level, wealth, and who your father is. The syllabus
is public; the test is written; the marker is external. That is the whole
filter.

## What membership entitles you to, and what it does not

**Entitles:** to speak for the platform; to vote in its internal processes;
to stand for its candidate selection; to be listed publicly as a member.

**Does not entitle:** to a position, a contract, a nomination, or a salary.
Passing an exam proves you understand the programme. It does not prove you
can govern, and we should never confuse the two — that error is precisely
what Chapter 2 warns about.

**Candidate selection is a separate process with its own standard**, and
membership is a precondition rather than a qualification.

## The honest risks

Recorded here because a standard that hides its failure modes is propaganda.

**Risk 1 — the exam becomes a gate for insiders.** Every credentialing body
drifts toward serving its own members. *Mitigation:* published pass rates by
state, free entry, external marking, and public syllabus. If pass rates skew
sharply toward one region or class, that is visible and must be answered.

**Risk 2 — it filters for the literate, not the committed.** A written exam
in a country with real illiteracy is a class filter, however carefully
designed. *Mitigation:* Pidgin and mother-tongue versions, oral examination
at Level 1 where needed, and an explicit non-member supporter tier so that a
person who cannot sit an exam is not excluded from the movement — only from
speaking in its name. **This risk is not fully solved and we say so.**

**Risk 3 — it slows growth.** It will. A platform that grows slowly and
knows what it believes beats one that grows fast and does not. But we should
not pretend the trade-off is costless, and if the movement needs numbers
faster than the exam produces them, that pressure will come and must be
refused in public.

**Risk 4 — we mark our own homework anyway.** The external examiner is the
safeguard, and an external examiner paid by us is not fully external. *This
is the same unresolved problem the Absorption Playbook found in every
Nigerian verification arrangement — every working case has a donor paying
the verifier.* We inherit the problem and have not solved it. **Owed.**

## What happens to a member who breaks the commitments

The four commitments in `00_WHAT_THIS_IS.md` bind members as well as the
platform. A member who states a claim above its evidence status, or who
attacks a person rather than an argument, is corrected in public. Repeated
breach ends membership.

**This applies most strictly to whoever leads.** A platform that disciplines
its members and not its founders is a personality vehicle with a rulebook.
