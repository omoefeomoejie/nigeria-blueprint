# Basic Education

*₦332 billion is sitting idle right now because states cannot or will not
take it, while twenty million children are out of school. Whatever this
problem is at the margin, it is not a shortage of money.*

*That single fact rearranges the conversation, including part of our own
Chapter 7.*

---

## 1. What the sector produces now

Between **18 and 20 million** Nigerian children are out of school by UNICEF
estimates — the largest such population in the world; 10.2 million of
primary age, 8.1 million junior secondary. One 2026 estimate from Save the
Children puts it near 28 million [Certified].

Against that, as of August 2026: states have accessed over **₦177 billion**
in UBE matching grants, while **more than ₦65 billion in counterpart funds
remains unaccessed and about ₦267 billion in already-released intervention
funds remains unutilised** [Certified — UBEC].

Not a one-year anomaly: **27 of 36 states failed to access UBEC matching
grants in 2024**, and in 2023 only ₦21bn of ₦51.6bn was drawn, by 16 states
— roughly **41% utilisation** [Certified — Plan International].

## 2. What is broken

**States cannot absorb money that is already theirs.** ₦332bn idle while
20 million children are out of school. UBEC's own framing: these resources
"cannot improve education while they remain in accounts."

**The grant is not free, and that is the mechanism.** Under the UBE Act,
states must provide **50% counterpart funding** to draw down federal
matching grants [Certified]. So accessing requires (a) cash the state must
find and (b) the project-preparation capacity to spend it properly. States
short of either leave the money.

**Completion is being counted instead of learning.** UBEC's Executive
Secretary states it directly: the real measure of success "is not the amount
of money accessed or spent, but the impact such investments make on the
lives and learning of Nigerian children," and states must move beyond
accessing funds to converting them into "measurable improvements in schools
and learning outcomes" [Certified].

**The gap is widest where need is greatest.** The states least able to
provide counterpart funding and prepare projects are, in general, the states
with the worst out-of-school numbers. The mechanism transfers money toward
capacity, not toward need.

## 3. Which horizontal failure explains each part

| Broken part | Horizontal failure | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Funds accessed but unspent; projects unprepared | Execution/conversion | Ch. 1 |
| Counted completions, not learning | Measurement absent | Ch. 4, Ch. 7 |
| Money flows to capacity, not need | No equity clause — Ceará's own failure mode | Ch. 7 |
| Persistent across administrations | Flow without statutory housing | Rule 7 |

**Fourth convergence — and a partial correction to Chapter 7.** Our
education chapter assumed the binding constraint was *measurement*: build
the assessment, tie transfers to it. This sector says the binding constraint
is at least equally **absorption**. You cannot tie a transfer to outcomes if
the recipient cannot process a transfer at all. Ceará's mechanism needs an
absorption fix bolted to its front end — technical assistance is not an
optional impact-doubler here, it is the entry condition.

**And a precondition may have just been met.** Chapter 7 stated that Nigeria
lacked the monitoring system Ceará's model requires. UBEC now reports the
successful conduct of a **National Learning Assessment**, with the Federal
Ministry of Education, explicitly to provide evidence for targeted
interventions [Certified]. If that assessment is credible and state-level
disaggregated, the Ceará transplant moves from theoretically possible to
immediately buildable. **Owed: verify its quality and granularity.**

## 4. What is genuinely sector-specific

**Matching-grant design punishes the poor state.** A 50% counterpart rule is
a capacity test disguised as a commitment test. Sectors funded this way
systematically underserve the states that need them most — this is a design
flaw in the funding instrument itself, not a governance failure of any
particular state.

**Idle money is politically invisible.** Unspent education funds generate no
protest, because nobody experiences a school that was never built. Compare
unpaid salaries, which produce strikes within weeks. Reforms must therefore
create the constituency that notices — publishing per-state access and
utilisation rates is the cheapest way to do it.

**Out-of-school numbers are contested** (18–20m vs 28m). A sector whose
headline statistic varies by ten million needs its own measurement fixed
before its targets mean anything.

## 5. What one state could try in 18 months

Powers required: state and SUBEB powers. Already held.

1. **Fix absorption first.** A standing project-preparation unit inside
   SUBEB whose only job is drawing down and deploying every naira the state
   is entitled to. Publish the access rate quarterly. This alone moves more
   money than any new budget line.
2. **Publish your own access and utilisation figures** — accessed,
   unaccessed, released-but-unspent — before anyone else does it for you.
3. **Adopt the National Learning Assessment as the state baseline** if it is
   credible; if not, commission an independent P2 literacy / P5 numeracy
   assessment (Chapter 7).
4. **Then tie LG transfers to the index** — Ceará's mechanism, with the
   equity clause, technical assistance to the bottom quartile, and the
   assessment published in full.
5. **Sequence matters:** absorption → measurement → incentive. Skipping to
   step 4 in a state that cannot draw down a grant will produce nothing.

**Kill condition.** If after 18 months access and utilisation rates have not
risen, the constraint is not incentive design but administrative capacity —
stop building index machinery and put the entire effort into project
preparation and staffing.
