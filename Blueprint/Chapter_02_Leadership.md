# Chapter 2 — Who Gets the Seat

*This chapter has the biggest hole in the book, and we would rather point at
it than paint over it. Nigeria has shown that merit selection can be carried
out. No Nigerian case has yet shown that it made services better, or that it
outlived the governor who started it. Both gaps shape everything below.*

---

## 1. Desired Outcome
By year 10, entry and promotion in at least one full service corridor
(teaching, revenue, or health) run on published instruments with published
thresholds; and the corridor's *service outcome* — not its dismissal count —
moves measurably against a published baseline.

## 2. Structural Matches
- **Kaduna 2017–2022** [Certified — execution only]: ~21,780 teachers
  dismissed on a primary-four benchmark, ~25,000 recruited by screening;
  repeated in 2021; survived an NLC strike, litigation, and dismissing the
  NUT's own national president.
- **Georgia 2004** [Certified — mechanism]: ~16,000 traffic police dismissed
  at once, force rebuilt, salaries raised up to fifteenfold, petty
  corruption effectively eliminated, police became the third most trusted
  institution — and it **survived succession**, because it was rebuilt as
  an institution, not run as a practice.
- **Kyrgyzstan post-2011** [Certified — failure]: copied Georgia's policy,
  lacked the preconditions (could not execute mass dismissals; no fiscal
  room), and failed.

## 3. The Mechanism

The transferable part is not "sack the bad ones." It is four things
together: a test published before anyone sits, a pass mark fixed in advance,
an institution that owns both, and pay high enough that the job is worth
keeping honestly.

Georgia raised police salaries up to fifteenfold. That was not generosity.
It was the other half of the mechanism. A man who cannot feed his family on
the salary will find the money somewhere, and no test catches that.

## 4. Preconditions (Rule 5)
Political mandate to absorb an organized strike; fiscal room for the pay
leg; and willingness to apply the test to allies. Kyrgyzstan proves that
without these, the same policy produces nothing.

## 5. Nigerian Constraints
Federal character constitutionalizes group allocation in appointments
[Certified, Node 2] — merit reform must be designed *alongside* it, not as
an open challenge to it, or it dies at a gate it cannot pass (Rule 9). And
the instrument itself is attackable: Kaduna's 2017 test drew an independent
audit finding computation errors and a 75% pass bar far above the West
African credit standard [Contested]. A contestable instrument hands the
loser their argument.

## 6. Nigerian Adaptation
**One corridor, statutory board, instrument published before anyone sits.**
- Instrument and threshold gazetted *in advance*, with an independent
  examiner and a published appeals process (closes the Kaduna instrument
  gap).
- The 40–74% band goes to *mandatory training*, not dismissal — Kaduna's
  own 2021 practice, and it splits the losing coalition.
- **Pay leg included or the reform is half-built** (Georgia's evidence).
- **Outcome measured from day one**: the corridor's service metric
  (learning, collection, patient throughput) baselined before the first
  test — this is the gap Node 2 could not fill, and no pilot may repeat it.

## 7. Political Economy — the outwaiting problem
The loser here is organized, permanent, and patient. Kaduna's NUT lost the
strike, lost in court, lost its president's job — **and won anyway**, by
waiting for the founder to leave: no competency test has surfaced since
2021, and the successor pivoted to retention and welfare [Certified].
Counter-design: statutory board with fixed tenure; testing calendar written
into law; a beneficiary constituency (parents' associations, service users)
organized *before* the first dismissal; and the pay leg to split the
workforce's own interest.

## 8. Early-Win Pilot
18 months, one corridor: board constituted by law; instrument gazetted;
baseline service metric published; first cohort assessed with the
train-don't-sack band operating; results and appeals published.

## 9. Scale Strategy
Publish corridor outcome metrics; let comparison drive adoption. Note the
sequencing insight from Node 2: examination-based **promotion** proved more
succession-proof in Kaduna than examination-based **dismissal** — start
where the reform survives.

## 10. Success Metrics
Year 1: board + instrument + baseline live. Year 3: service metric moved
measurably; testing cycle completed at least twice **under two different
administrations if a handover falls in the window**. Year 10: Outcome (§1).

## 11. Kill Condition
If by year 3 the workforce has been screened but the service metric has not
moved, the premise — that selection drives delivery — has failed in this
corridor. Stop; redirect to training and management reform. This chapter's
whole vulnerability is that Nigeria has never yet measured this link.

## Irreversibility Test (Rule 7) — the cautionary case
**Stock:** the screened workforce, the recruits, the published instrument.
*Kaduna proves this persists — the dismissals were never reversed.*
**Flow:** the testing cycle itself. *Kaduna proves this dies* — unhoused,
undefended, gone at succession. Georgia proves it can live — housed as an
institution with a public constituency. **This chapter exists because of
that contrast.**

### Scorekeeper note

A state that sets its own competency test, marks its own scripts and
announces its own result is keeping its own score — the same structure that
makes state-run local elections uncompetitive (Chapter 8). Kaduna's 2017
instrument drew an independent audit finding computation errors and a pass
mark far above the West African standard, which is what that structure
produces.

So the independent examiner in §6 is not a procedural nicety. **It is what
makes devolved selection legitimate at all.** Without it, this chapter is
recommending that governors grade themselves.

---

### Cost
**₦150m–₦500m capital, one test cycle a year recurring.** Cheaper than it looks, because states already run computer-based recruitment tests through accredited JAMB centres — Adamawa used 13 statewide in 2025; Oyo and Ekiti ran theirs through university and polytechnic centres [Certified]. A state does not need to build testing capacity; it rents it. The money goes on instrument design, the independent examiner, centre hire and publication.

**The independent examiner is the item not to economise on.** It is what makes the result legitimate (see the scorekeeper note above). Full working: `COSTING.md`.
