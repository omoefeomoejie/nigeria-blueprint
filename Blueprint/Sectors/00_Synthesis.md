# What Six Sectors Proved

*We opened the vertical axis to test the diagnosis, not to illustrate it.
Six sectors later, the diagnosis survived — but it did not survive
unchanged. This is what the briefs collectively established, and what they
cost us.*

---

## 1. The test we set

Part II claims a first principle: the Nigerian state lacks the capacity and
incentives to act consistently in citizens' long-term interests. Seven
horizontal investigations supported it — but we chose those seven, which
means they could have been chosen to fit.

The sector axis was the check. If independent sectors decomposed into the
same root causes, the diagnosis would be confirmed by convergence from
starting points we did not select. If a sector failed for reasons the
horizontals could not explain, Layer 4 would have to change.

Six sectors ran. All six converged. But three of them also changed
something.

## 2. What the sectors confirmed

Every sector decomposed into failures the horizontal chapters already named:

| Sector | Dominant horizontal failure |
|---|---|
| Agriculture | Leakage (Ch. 1 inverted); capture by the already-strong (Ch. 7); nothing measured (Ch. 4) |
| Power | Capacity absent behind new authority; no measurement (Ch. 4) |
| Primary Health | Refurbishment ≠ delivery (Ch. 1); last-mile traceability (Ch. 4) |
| Basic Education | Funds unabsorbed and unspent (Ch. 1); completions counted, not learning (Ch. 7) |
| Water | Assets unmaintained; utilities not collecting (Ch. 1); no owning constituency (Rule 7) |
| Transport | Money released, nothing delivered; no post-disbursement consequence (Ch. 4) |

The most striking confirmations came from people outside this project:
- A sector economist: Nigeria's agricultural deficit is **"not a shortage of
  proven technologies"** but institutional failure across seed, credit,
  insurance, extension, market access and land tenure.
- BudgIT's Tracka, after visiting 2,760 projects: **"This is no longer a
  funding problem… That points clearly to failures after disbursement."**
- UBEC's Executive Secretary: success is **"not the amount of money accessed
  or spent, but the impact… on the lives and learning of Nigerian
  children."**

Three institutions with no relationship to this Blueprint, describing its
Layer 4 in their own words.

## 3. What the sectors changed

**One addition to the diagnosis — absorptive capacity (Layer 3).**
Three sectors produced the same failure across three different resources:
authority (16 states took electricity oversight they could not operate),
money (₦332bn of education funding unaccessed or unspent; 27 of 36 states
accessed nothing in 2024), infrastructure (water utilities produce in 28
states, function in 16; 40% of boreholes fail within two years).

*The state frequently cannot absorb, operate or maintain the resources and
authority it already holds.* Giving a low-absorption state more money or
more powers does not produce delivery — it produces idle accounts, dormant
regulators and decaying assets, and sometimes a worse outcome than before
the transfer.

**One refinement that matters more than the finding — absorption is a
distribution, not a condition, and organisational before geographic.**
Independent tracking found **97.5% of abandoned-but-paid-for projects in
five states**, and — the sharper cut — a **62-point completion spread
between federal agencies** (UBEC 78%, Agriculture 68%, REA 65%; Lower Benue
River Basin 20%, Niger Delta Affairs 16%). Same country, same contractors,
same law, same year.

*(Corrected: an earlier draft said "seventeen states with none." Only Cross
River recorded no abandoned, unexecuted or fraudulently delivered projects;
top-ten completion rates run ~52–63%; non-execution concentrates in a
different five states. The narrower claim is the accurate one.)*

This is the single most useful fact the vertical axis produced. A national
condition invites fatalism; a distribution invites ranking; ranking invites
competition — the scaling mechanism every chapter here already depends on.
The question stops being *can Nigerians execute* and becomes *what do the
seventeen do that the five do not.*

**Two corrections to our own chapters.**
- *Chapter 5* said take every open constitutional door. Power showed that an
  acquired power without capacity degrades governance. Corrected: take the
  door **and build the capacity first** (Mistakes 8.1).
- *Chapter 7* said the binding constraint was measurement. Education showed
  absorption binds at least as hard — you cannot tie a transfer to outcomes
  if the recipient cannot process a transfer. Corrected sequence:
  **absorption → measurement → incentive.**

## 4. What the sectors taught that no chapter had said

**Nigeria's recurring failure is keeping, not building.** Boreholes fail at
40% in two years. A renovated PHC is destroyed by erosion. 56,000 projects
sit abandoned. Maintenance appears in no budget line, and every sector pays
for it. If one sentence had to carry the vertical axis, it would be this
one.

**Where exit is cheap, the reform constituency dissolves.** 75% of
Nigeria's 2.31 million water points are self-supplied. Households that drill
their own boreholes never lobby for municipal water again — and they are
disproportionately the households with the loudest voices. The same logic
explains generators, private schools and private clinics. **Every sector
where the middle class can exit loses its advocates first**, which is why
those sectors decay quietly. Reform must recruit the exited deliberately.

**Sector failures cascade.** A "functional" primary health centre requires
reliable power and water. Health's ceiling is therefore set by power's floor
and water's floor. No sector plan is independent of the others, and
sequencing across sectors matters as much as sequencing within them.

**Idle money makes no noise.** Unspent education funds produce no protest,
because nobody experiences a school that was never built — while unpaid
salaries produce strikes in weeks. Publishing per-state access and
utilisation rates is the cheapest reform available anywhere in this
Blueprint, because it manufactures a constituency that would otherwise never
form.

## 5. What this means for the Blueprint

1. **Capacity is not a precondition to be assumed. It is the reform.** Every
   chapter's pilot must now begin with an absorption check.
2. **Rank, don't lament.** State-level absorption is measurable. Publish the
   league table; competitive federalism does the rest.
3. **Budget maintenance as a named line** in every sector proposal.
4. **Sequence across sectors**, not only within them: power and water
   underwrite health and education.
5. **Recruit the exited** wherever private substitution is widespread.

## 6. What we did not do, and why

Two planned briefs (housing & land administration, trade & investment) and
oil & gas were not written. After six sectors the horizontal explanations
had begun repeating and the marginal finding was falling; completing eight
for symmetry would have been warehouse-building, which this project exists
to refuse. They remain available if a specific question requires them.

**Owed:** whether the concentration holds across other datasets and years;
and **what UBEC does that the 16%-completion agencies do not** — now the
most valuable unrun investigation in this repository, and more tractable
than the state version because the unit is a single organisation with a
public leadership, published reports, and a documented method.
