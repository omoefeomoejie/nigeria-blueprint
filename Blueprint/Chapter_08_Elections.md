# Chapter 8 — Counting: Closing the Window

*The machines worked. The counting worked. What failed was the journey
between the polling unit where your vote was counted and the centre where
the number was announced. That journey is the whole problem, and it is
measured in minutes.*

---

## 1. Desired Outcome
By the 2027 cycle, every polling unit result is uploaded before the officials
leave the unit, while party agents and voters are still standing there, and
the uploaded record carries legal weight in court.
Baseline: in 2023, only **31.8% of polling unit results** reached the
viewing portal within 36 hours, and collation centres in Lagos, Rivers,
Kogi and several northern states announced figures differing materially from
what the machines recorded at the units [Certified].

## 2. Structural Matches
Nigeria's own two cycles are the comparison. 2023: technology deployed
without statutory duty. 2026 Act: statutory duty created, timing left
contested. There is no need to look abroad for this chapter; the natural
experiment is domestic and recent.

## 3. The Mechanism

Not the machine. The window.

Between the counting at your polling unit and the announcement at the
collation centre, paper travels. In that gap, numbers can change. Upload the
result at the unit, in front of the agents and the crowd, and the gap
closes. Upload it later, "in a manner prescribed," and the gap stays open.

The committee clause that would have required immediate upload — while
agents and citizens were still present to verify — is exactly this
mechanism. It was voted down on 4 February 2026, restored in an emergency
session on 10 February after nationwide protest, and the final text is read
differently by credible analysts [Contested].

## 4. Preconditions
Network coverage, contested and checkable: the President cited broadband
limits and hacking risk; telecom operators replied with regulator data
showing **over 70% nationwide 3G/4G coverage** [Certified]. Also required:
devices preserved as evidence, which failed in 2023 when accreditation
machines were reconfigured between February and March, destroying their
evidential value before petitions were heard [Certified].

## 5. Nigerian Constraints
The courts have been explicit. Under the 2022 Act the portal was "merely a
public viewing portal" with no legal force, and a petitioner could not rely
on it to prove over-voting [Certified]. That is the cost of building a
transparency tool administratively instead of legislating it. Whatever
Nigeria builds next must live in statute or it will not survive a court.

## 6. Nigerian Adaptation
1. **Legislate the timing, not just the transmission.** Upload at the unit,
   before officials depart, with agents present.
2. **Make the uploaded record admissible** in election petitions.
3. **Freeze accreditation devices** from the close of polls until petitions
   are exhausted; criminalise reconfiguration.
4. **Publish upload rates live** on election day, by state, so failure is
   visible while it is happening rather than argued about afterwards.
5. **Define technical failure precisely** — what counts, who certifies it,
   what the fallback is, and what the deadline for late upload is.

## 7. Political Economy
The losers are whoever benefits from the gap between unit and centre. Note
how they defended it: not by opposing transmission, which is popular, but by
amending **when** it must happen. That is a defeat by adverb, and it is
harder to protest than a repeal because the reform still appears to pass.

Counter-strategy: campaign on the timing word specifically. "Upload before
they leave the unit" is a sentence a market woman can hold, and it is the
only version of the reform that binds.

## 8. Early-Win Pilot
Any off-cycle governorship election before 2027: publish live upload rates
by local government, hour by hour, with civil society mirroring the data.
No legislation needed to *observe*, and observation creates the record.

## 9. Scale Strategy
Publish the state-by-state upload rate after every election. A number that
embarrasses is a number that improves.

## 10. Success Metrics
2027: upload rate above 95% within two hours of close of poll; zero material
divergence between unit records and collation announcements; uploaded
records accepted as evidence in petitions.

## 11. Kill Condition
If upload rates rise but collation figures still diverge from unit records,
the window is not the binding constraint and the problem sits in collation
itself — redirect to collation-centre observation and audit.

## Irreversibility Test (Rule 7) — this chapter's own lesson
**Stock:** the devices, the trained officials, the registered voters.
**Flow:** the duty to upload, its timing, and its legal weight. In 2023 the
flow had no statutory home, and the courts correctly held there was no
obligation to breach. **This chapter exists because a transparency
mechanism without a statute is a courtesy, and courtesies fail under
pressure.**

## Addendum — the tier nobody watches

Everything above concerns national elections. The local elections that
choose the government closest to you are run by state commissions, and the
numbers are these: in 2025, Lagos's ruling party won 375 of 376
councillorship seats; Osun's ruling party won all 332; Ondo's won all 203.
Two different parties, three states, the same outcome. The INEC chairman
himself calls these polls "mere coronation of candidates of ruling parties"
[Certified].

The reason is not that the people who run them are worse people. It is that
state electoral commissions depend on the governor for their appointments
and their money. No commission funded by a man can declare against him.

**So this chapter's one recommendation that cuts against the rest of the
book: local government elections should be run by INEC, not by the states.**
The proof already exists — INEC runs council elections in the FCT, and the
opposition has won there.

Everywhere else in this Blueprint we argue for pushing power down, because
competition between states makes them better. It only works when somebody
else keeps the score. Hand a governor the referee and you do not get
thirty-six competing states. You get thirty-six monopolies.

### Cost
**Not a state cost.** Real-time upload is federal, legislative, and partly legislated already. A state's contribution is political pressure, and civic mirroring of upload rates on election day — which civil society can do for the price of a spreadsheet and a phone tree. Full working: `COSTING.md`.
