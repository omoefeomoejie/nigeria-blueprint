# Housing & Land Administration

*Nigerians built these houses with their own money. The state's only job was
to write down who owns them, and it charges a third of the value to do it.
Three hundred billion dollars is frozen behind that desk.*

---

## 1. What the sector produces now

More than **90% of Nigerian land is unregistered and untitled**, though it
is traded constantly [Certified — FMHUD via CGIAR]. At household level the
NBS Living Standards Survey puts it plainly: **71.4% of landlords hold no
document at all**, 13.2% hold a title deed, and **8.1% hold a Certificate of
Occupancy** [Certified — NBS].

The federal housing ministry estimates the consequence at **$300 billion in
dead capital, roughly 60% of national GDP** — assets that cannot be traded
lawfully or pledged as collateral because nobody wrote them down
[Certified].

And note who built the housing stock: about **90% of Nigerian houses are
built from the owner's own savings** [Certified].

## 2. What is broken

**The state charges people to make their own property usable.**
Registration, governor's consent, stamp duties and associated charges can
reach **15–30% of the property's value** in some states, and the process
runs from months to years [Certified].

Read that as a citizen. You built the house. To make it legally yours in a
way a bank recognises, the state wants up to a third of what it is worth.
So you do not register, and your house stays a house instead of becoming
capital.

**The Land Use Act put every governor at the centre.** All land is held in
trust by the governor, who grants rights and issues certificates. Critics
identify the consequences: delay, inconsistent implementation, and excessive
administrative control over ordinary transactions [Certified].

**Where the state has tried, the failure is capacity, not law.** The KADGIS
study in Kaduna names the obstacles from the households' side: ignorance of
the process, cost, inefficient registration, corruption, and too few skilled
staff to run the system [Certified].

**Digitisation is happening and is not enough.** Lagos, Abuja and other
states have expanded registry digitisation, and analysts still report
implementation gaps and weak institutional coordination [Certified].

## 3. Which horizontal failure explains each part

| Broken part | Horizontal failure | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Fees priced as a revenue event | Collection thinking, applied to the wrong thing | Ch. 1 |
| Registry exists but does not reach people | Absorption: the system cannot process its own citizens | Part II, Layer 3 |
| Digitised and still slow | Stock built, flow never staffed | Rule 7 |
| Process opaque, outcome uncertain | No published service standard | Ch. 4 |

**Seventh convergence — and the inversion that makes this sector different.**
Every other brief describes a state failing to *deliver* something. This one
describes a state **blocking citizens from using what they already own.**
The houses exist. The owners exist. Only the paperwork is missing, and the
paperwork is the state's single job here.

**The cross-node payoff, and it is large.** Every state wants property tax.
Property tax needs a register. The register is empty because registration is
priced as a one-off revenue event rather than as public infrastructure.

> **Cut the fee toward zero and the register fills. A full register is a
> permanent tax base. States are taxing the doorway instead of the room.**

Lagos's 800,000 enumerated properties [Node 1] are the beginning of exactly
this, and the reason Lagos can tax property at all.

## 4. What is genuinely sector-specific

**The asset already exists.** Unlike power, water or schools, nothing needs
building. The value is already there and legally frozen. This is the only
sector in the Blueprint where the entire intervention is administrative.

**Formalisation has losers among the informal winners.** Land touts,
"omo onile" networks, and intermediaries who profit from opacity lose
directly when a register makes ownership checkable. This is a
political-economy fight at street level rather than in a ministry.

**Titling is not the same as tenure security for everyone.** A Certificate
of Occupancy is evidence of a right of occupancy, not freehold ownership.
And systematic mapping that captures only private parcels can erase common
resources, seasonal use rights, grazing corridors and water points — with
direct consequences for pastoralist–farmer conflict [Certified]. **A titling
programme designed without commons mapping can manufacture violence.**
Women's tenure rights are similarly at risk where registration defaults to
the male household head.

## 5. What one state could try in 18 months

Powers required: state land powers under the Land Use Act. Already held.

1. **Cut the total cost of first registration to a token fee** for
   owner-occupied residential property below a set value. Treat the register
   as infrastructure, not as a revenue line. The recurring property tax is
   the return.
2. **Publish a service standard** — days to title, cost, and the current
   backlog, updated monthly. Nobody publishes this.
3. **Systematic ward-by-ward titling**, state-initiated, rather than
   waiting for individual applications from people who do not know the
   process exists.
4. **Map the commons in the same exercise** — grazing corridors, water
   points, seasonal use rights — and register women as co-holders by
   default on household property.
5. **Publish the register.** A searchable public record is what turns a
   title into collateral a bank will accept, and what removes the tout from
   the transaction.

**Kill condition.** If registration volumes do not rise sharply after fees
are cut, cost was not the binding constraint — it is process time or public
awareness, and the effort moves there. If volumes rise but bank lending
against titles does not follow within three years, the constraint is the
credit system rather than the register, and this chapter has been solved
without the intended benefit arriving.
