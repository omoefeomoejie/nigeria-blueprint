# The Scorekeeper Test, Applied to All Eight Nodes

**The test** (earned at Node 8, SIEC extension): *before recommending
devolution in any domain, ask who keeps the score. If the unit being
devolved to also keeps its own score, devolution produces monopoly rather
than competition, and the prescription inverts to centralisation.*

**Why this file exists.** The red team's Tier 1.3 finding: we discovered
that boundary **by accident, in the eighth node**. A test found by luck has
told us nothing about how many other boundaries exist. This applies it
deliberately, node by node.

---

## The test, run

### Node 1 — Revenue
**Who keeps the score?** NBS publishes IGR figures. BudgIT computes autonomy
and transparency indices. FAAC allocations are federal and published.
**Verdict: PASSES.** The state generates the revenue and an outside party
counts it. Competitive federalism works here because a governor cannot
mark his own homework — which is precisely why the league tables in
Chapters 1 and 4 function as a scaling mechanism.
**Caveat found:** the *inputs* to those tables are partly state-supplied
(Enugu's ESIRS figures). The scorer is independent; the raw data is not
always. Weaker pass than it first appears.

### Node 2 — Leadership selection
**Who keeps the score?** The state sets the test, sets the pass mark, marks
the scripts, and announces the result. Kaduna's 2017 instrument drew an
independent audit finding computation errors and a pass mark far above the
West African standard.
**Verdict: FAILS the test as currently designed.** This is a second
boundary, and we did not previously name it as one.
**But it is fixable, unlike elections.** Chapter 2 already requires a
gazetted instrument, a fixed threshold published in advance, an independent
examiner and a published appeals process. Those provisions convert a failing
domain into a passing one. **Recommendation: state explicitly in Chapter 2
that the independent examiner is not a nicety — it is what makes devolved
selection legitimate at all.**

### Node 3 — Security
**Who keeps the score?** Crime statistics are collected by the same
authorities responsible for crime. Node 3 already found attribution
impossible for Amotekun with public data.
**Verdict: FAILS.** Third boundary. A state security outfit reporting its
own effectiveness is the classic case.
**Chapter 3 already partly answers it** by requiring an independent
complaints line published monthly and a comparison against a non-adopting
state. **Strengthen: incident data should be published raw, by ward, and
mirrored by a civic body — not summarised by the outfit itself.**

### Node 4 — Accountability
**Who keeps the score?** BudgIT's transparency league; OGP's Independent
Reporting Mechanism; civil society co-chairs inside the state's own OGP
structure.
**Verdict: PASSES, and it is the strongest pass in the corpus.** This is why
the accountability machinery survived succession in Kaduna while the
competency testing did not — an external scorer creates an external
constituency.

### Node 5 — Constitutional
**Who keeps the score?** Nobody, and that is the finding. The gate-holder
decides whether a proposal is even transmitted, and no external body records
the failure. RMAFC's proposals have died five times with no scoreboard.
**Verdict: FAILS at the centre, not at the unit.** A novel inversion: here
the *centre* keeps its own score, which is why Chapter 5's keystone is a
statutory transmission deadline — a rule that creates a scoreboard where
none exists.

### Node 6 — National identity
**Who keeps the score?** Afrobarometer, on a multi-country instrument, plus
peer-reviewed causal work (Okunogbe on NYSC).
**Verdict: PASSES, strongly.** No Nigerian government controls the
measurement. Chapter 6's targets are therefore safe to state publicly.

### Node 7 — Education
**Who keeps the score?** Ceará's model requires an independent assessment;
Nigeria's National Learning Assessment is federally run, and the states
being measured are not the measurers.
**Verdict: PASSES, conditionally.** The condition is that the assessment
stays independent of the states whose transfers depend on it. **If a state
ever runs the assessment that determines its own local governments'
money, the whole Chapter 7 mechanism inverts into Node 8's failure.**
Written into Chapter 7 as a design constraint.

### Node 8 — Elections
**Who keeps the score?** The governor appoints and funds the commission that
declares the result.
**Verdict: FAILS totally.** Lagos 375/376, Osun 332/332, Ondo 203/203,
across two parties. Already recorded; this is the case that generated the
test.

## What the deliberate application found

**Three failures, not one.** Elections (known), leadership selection (new),
and security (new). The red team's suspicion was correct: finding one
boundary by accident meant others were unexamined.

**And the failures share a shape.** In all three, the devolved unit
generates the *thing being measured* and also *does the measuring*: a state
tests its own teachers, a security outfit reports its own crime figures, a
commission declares its own elections.

**The passes share a shape too.** In every passing domain, an outside body
with its own funding and reputation does the counting — NBS, BudgIT,
Afrobarometer, OGP, a federal assessment. **Competitive federalism is not a
property of devolution. It is a property of external measurement.** Devolve
without an external scorer and you get thirty-six monopolies; devolve with
one and you get a league table.

**A refinement to the test itself.** Two of the three failures are
*fixable* — selection and security can be made passing domains by inserting
an independent examiner or a civic data mirror. Elections cannot, because
the scorer *is* the institution. So the test now has two outcomes rather
than one:

> **Fails and fixable** → insert an external scorer, then devolve.
> **Fails structurally** → do not devolve; centralise.

That distinction is new, and it softens Node 8's prescription from a blanket
warning into a design instruction.

---

## Changes this forces

1. **Chapter 2:** the independent examiner is load-bearing, not optional.
   State it.
2. **Chapter 3:** raw incident data published by ward and mirrored by a
   civic body, not summarised by the outfit.
3. **Chapter 7:** a design constraint — the assessment must never be run by
   a state whose LG transfers depend on it.
4. **Chapter 1 caveat:** the scorer is independent, the raw inputs often are
   not. Enugu's ESIRS figures are the live example.
5. **Assumptions.md:** units-first now has three boundaries, two fixable.

# Part Two — the test run against the eight sector briefs

## The test, run

### Sector 01 — Agriculture
**Who keeps the score?** Yields are measured by NAERLS (federal) and by
international bodies (FAO, USDA projections). Fertiliser and extension
coverage come from IFPRI stakeholder surveys.
**Verdict: PASSES.** The state does not measure its own harvest.
**Caveat:** a state-run farmer registry (§5) *would* be state-scored. Keep
the registry state-run for delivery and the yield baseline independent for
measurement — do not let one body do both.

### Sector 02 — Power
**Who keeps the score?** NERC records transfers; supply and metering data
sit with DisCos and the regulator. After devolution, **the state regulator
licenses the operators and also reports on the market it created.**
**Verdict: FAILS, and this explains the two-year gap.** Sixteen states took
oversight and "little meaningful improvement in supply" is reported — but
nobody can verify improvement or its absence, because the new scorer is the
new regulator. The absence of a baseline is not an oversight; it is the
predictable output of a domain that fails this test.
**Fixable:** Chapter/Sector 02's pilot already demands a published supply
baseline by feeder. Strengthen: baseline published to a national standard
NERC still maintains, so it is comparable across states.

### Sector 03 — Primary health
**Who keeps the score?** NPHCDA runs the dashboard; Nigeria Health Watch
computed the 7.3% figure from it independently.
**Verdict: PASSES, and it is the cleanest pass in the sector axis.** The
publisher was federal, the analyst was independent, and the number
embarrassed the programme. That is the mechanism working exactly as
designed.
**Note:** the state is not the scorer here because basic health data is
federally aggregated. If health data were state-aggregated, this would
invert.

### Sector 04 — Basic education
**Who keeps the score?** UBEC publishes access and utilisation figures per
state; the National Learning Assessment is federally run.
**Verdict: PASSES.** The states being scored are not the scorers.
**This is why publishing per-state access rates is the cheapest reform
available** (Sector 04 §4): the scoring infrastructure already exists.

### Sector 05 — Water & sanitation
**Who keeps the score?** WASHNORM, run with NBS and UNICEF — independent.
But *functionality* of a specific utility is reported by the utility.
**Verdict: SPLIT — passes at national level, fails at facility level.**
The national picture (28 states producing, 16 functional; 40% borehole
failure) is independently measured. Whether a given scheme works is
self-reported.
**Fixable, and cheaply:** the functionality register in §5 must be verified
by the community using the water point, not by the agency that built it.
This is the plural-verification design from the Absorption Playbook, applied
at the smallest possible scale.

### Sector 06 — Transport & capital projects
**Who keeps the score?** BudgIT's Tracka physically visits sites. The
Budget Office reports implementation; the Finance Ministry disputed it with
a different measure.
**Verdict: PASSES — and it is the corpus's best illustration of why.** The
17.66% and the 76–84% figures conflict precisely because two different
bodies measured. That conflict is not a weakness in the evidence; it is the
external scorer doing its job. Where only the ministry measured, there would
have been one number and no argument.

### Sector 07 — Housing & land
**Who keeps the score?** The state runs the land registry, sets the fees,
processes the applications, and reports the results. Nobody independent
counts titles issued or measures time-to-title.
**Verdict: FAILS.** As predicted. There is no BudgIT for land registries.
**Fixable, and this is the actionable finding:** §5 already proposes
publishing a service standard — days to title, cost, backlog — which no
state currently does. Add: **that standard must be audited by a
professional body** (the Nigerian Institution of Estate Surveyors and
Valuers is the obvious candidate and already publishes on dead capital).
Without an external scorer, "we digitised the registry" is unfalsifiable,
which is precisely what analysts report about existing digitisation efforts.

### Sector 09 — Oil & gas
**Who keeps the score?** FAAC and NBS publish derivation receipts; BudgIT
analyses them. But **what states do with the money is not scored at all** —
which is how ₦10tn passed through with communities still poor.
**Verdict: SPLIT — inflow scored, outflow unscored.** The worst possible
configuration: the money is visible arriving and invisible thereafter.
**The PIA's Host Community Development Trusts are the structural answer**
(75% projects / 20% investment / 5% administration, statutory), because they
create a spending rule an outsider can check. Whether anyone checks it is
the watch item.

## What Part Two adds

**Two clean failures (power, housing), two splits (water, oil), four
passes.** Combined with Part One: **five failures across sixteen domains
tested.**

**The pattern holds and sharpens.** Every failure is a domain where the
state built the thing *and* reports on the thing. Every pass has a federal
agency, a civic organisation, or an international survey doing the counting.

**New observation — the "no BudgIT" problem.** Nigeria has strong external
scorers for money (BudgIT, Tracka), health (NPHCDA + Nigeria Health Watch),
identity (Afrobarometer) and agriculture (NAERLS, FAO). It has none for
**land registries**, **state electricity regulators**, or **facility-level
water functionality**. Those are exactly the three domains that failed.

> **The map of Nigeria's governance failures is close to the map of its
> missing scorekeepers.** Where a civic auditor exists, reform is visible
> and contestable. Where none exists, "we digitised it" is unfalsifiable.

**Which implies a cheap, high-leverage intervention nobody has proposed:**
funding or founding an external scorer in an unscored domain may do more for
that domain than any programme inside it. A land-registry equivalent of
BudgIT would cost a fraction of a single housing scheme and would make every
subsequent claim checkable.

**Correction to Sector 02's own account.** We wrote that the 2023 electricity
devolution produced "no measurable supply improvement." Strictly, the
correct statement is: **no measurable anything**, because the devolution
removed the scorer along with the authority. That is a sharper and more
damning finding, and it is now the first thing Sector 02 should say.
