# Chapter 3 — Security: The People Who Know the Ground

*Claim statuses as in Chapter 1.*

---

## 1. Desired Outcome
By year 10, participating states show sustained reductions in violent
incidents and kidnappings against a published baseline, with response times
and case clearance measured and published — and no increase in
security-actor abuse complaints, independently recorded.
Baseline reality [Certified]: state police are prohibited (ss. 214–215);
federal forces carry the monopoly; ~75% of national fatalities occur in the
North.

## 2. Structural Matches
- **Borno / Civilian JTF** [Certified — mechanism]: locals expelled Boko
  Haram from Maiduguri at 100+ member deaths; within-case contrast —
  comparable LGAs without a strong community organ were overrun.
- **Amotekun (South West)** [Contested]: perception of safety improved
  significantly in study data while regional fatalities rose faster than the
  national trend; attribution unresolvable with public data.
- **Georgia's police rebuild** [Certified, Node 2 crossover]: petty
  corruption eliminated; trust rebuilt; survived succession — the
  institutional-housing lesson, not the mass-dismissal tactic.

## 3. The Mechanism

Not more men with guns. The thing that won Maiduguri was knowing who people
are. Federal soldiers posted far from home do not speak the language, do not
know the terrain, and cannot tell a neighbour from an infiltrator
[Certified]. Local men could.

So the mechanism is a community layer that gathers what only locals know and
hands it to lawful force. Not a parallel army. An eye, not a gun.

## 4. Preconditions (Rule 5)
Georgia's rebuild needed a revolution-scale mandate, fiscal room for
salary multiples, and willingness to prosecute one's own side; Kyrgyzstan
copied the policy without them and failed [Certified, Node 2]. CJTF's
mechanism arose under *existential* threat — whether it generalizes to
ordinary crime is **Owed**.

## 5. Nigerian Constraints
The constitutional ceiling (ss. 214–215): states may fund and complement,
never own force [Certified]. Abuse and militia-drift are documented, not
hypothetical — CJTF abuses against civilians are recorded in the same
literature that certifies its effectiveness [Certified]. Demobilization
after the emergency is an unsolved problem.

## 6. Nigerian Adaptation
A **statutory State Community Intelligence Service**: recruited locally,
vetted, trained, salaried, *unarmed by default*, with a legal duty to route
intelligence to federal/joint forces — sitting under the constitutional
ceiling rather than pretending it isn't there. Powers: gather, report,
patrol jointly. Not: detain, try, or punish.

## 7. Political Economy
Losers: those profiting from insecurity's economy (ransom chains, protection
rackets, complicit officers) and any federal actor reading this as police
devolution by the back door. Counter-design: legislate it as *complementary*
and say so loudly; publish the routing obligation; build the federal-force
partnership publicly. Rule 6 warning: the organized loser will outwait a
founder — statutory housing from day one, never a governor's outfit.

## 8. Early-Win Pilot
One state, 18 months: statutory service stood up in three LGAs with a
published incident baseline, a joint-patrol protocol with the federal
command, and an independent complaints line whose numbers are published
monthly — the abuse metric ships *with* the crime metric, not after.

## 9. Scale Strategy
Publish a state security-performance table (incidents, clearance, response
time, complaints upheld) so replication is driven by comparison. Where the
constitutional door opens further, take it — but the model must work
*under* the current ceiling first [Rule 9, Node 5].

## 10. Success Metrics
Year 1: baseline + service operating + complaints line live. Year 3:
measurable incident reduction vs baseline and vs a comparable non-adopting
state (Rule 8's counterfactual contrast, built in from the start). Year 10:
Outcome (§1) met.

## 11. Kill Condition
If by year 3 incidents have not moved against the comparison state, **or**
upheld abuse complaints rise, stop. An abusive or ineffective community
force is worse than none — Node 3's evidence cuts both ways and this
chapter refuses to hide it.

## Irreversibility Test (Rule 7)
**Stock:** the trained personnel, the vetting registry, the routing
protocols. **Flow:** the service's statutory existence, its funding line,
its complaints mechanism, and the published performance table — each must
be in law with a named constituency (the joint federal command, the
communities served, and the civic auditors), or it dies with the governor
who created it.

### Weight note (from the interdependence map, v0.2)

This chapter carries more weight than it claims. The sector work found
security sitting underneath agriculture and transport as a hard dependency:
**2.1 million hectares of northern farmland inaccessible** (Niger 350,000
ha, Zamfara 323,000, Borno 300,000, Katsina 210,000, Benue 200,000), nearly
half of it inside a maize belt producing 44% of national maize; FAO puts
national food production down 15% from insecurity; the Northern Governors
Forum admits up to 60% of farmland in key agricultural states is abandoned
or underutilised [Certified].

Second-order effects compound it: animal traction costs rose in 2024 because
cattle theft made work animals scarce, and haulage now requires armed
escorts, contributing to 30–40 million tonnes of food lost annually
(₦3.5–5tn).

**What follows.** In the North West, North East and conflict-affected Middle
Belt, no agricultural, water or power intervention works before this
chapter does. And the constitutional ceiling means the community-
intelligence layer in §6 is the only lawful instrument a governor holds.
It is therefore not a modest supplement to federal policing. In those
regions it is the entry condition for every other chapter in this book.

**And it cannot work alone.** The loop runs both ways. Insecurity closes
farmland; closed farmland idles young men; idle young men are vulnerable to
recruitment — a 420-respondent survey across Zamfara, Katsina and Sokoto
confirms unemployment significantly raises that vulnerability [Certified].
Clear a ward without giving those men something to do and the pool refills
behind you.

**What livelihood work can and cannot do.** One source reports recruitment
at ₦5,000 [Contested — corroboration attempted, not found]. The economics
that did corroborate are larger: ransom, at around ₦1m per abducted student
in one documented case, with participants reporting they joined because it
paid better than herding [Certified]. For comparison, a police constable
earns roughly ₦54,000–62,000 a month [Certified].

So livelihood work is **necessary and insufficient**. It removes the
vulnerability recruiters exploit; it does not outbid a ransom share.
Security work removes the earnings; livelihood work removes the pool.

This chapter's pilot (§8) should therefore run jointly with Sector 01's
farmer registry in the same wards, under one budget line — for
complementarity, not because recruitment is cheap to outbid.
