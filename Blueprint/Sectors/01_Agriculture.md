# Agriculture

*Nobody feels "misaligned incentives." Everybody feels that the fertiliser
never came.*

---

## 1. What the sector produces now

Agriculture employs more Nigerians than anything else, and it runs on
smallholders. The headline number that explains most of the rest: fertiliser
application sits at roughly **13 kg per hectare**, against the 200 kg/ha the
FAO recommends [Certified — IFPRI/FMARD].

Extension services — the people who are supposed to tell a farmer what to
plant and how — reach **fewer than half of all smallholder farmers**
[Certified — Aremu and Reynolds 2024, via VoxDev].

## 2. What is broken

Four separate things, usually discussed as one.

**Inputs leak before they arrive.** Subsidised fertiliser has repeatedly
ended up in the open market instead of with the farmers it was meant for.
IFPRI names this leakage as a key obstacle not just to farmers but to the
private fertiliser sector ever developing [Certified]. The scale of the
absurdity: subsidy rates have reached **87%** while consumption stayed low
[Certified].

**Demand was never the problem.** IFPRI asked 44 people across the sector:
ministry officials, agency staff, input dealers, farmer associations, and
farmers with no association at all. All 44 said the same thing. Demand far
outstrips supply every year, and many farmers would pay market price if the
fertiliser were simply there [Certified].

Read that again. Nigeria has spent decades and subsidy rates as high as 87%
solving a demand problem that does not exist. The farmers were never the
obstacle. The distribution was.

**Help goes to those who least need it.** Extension access is positively
associated with asset ownership, fertiliser use, and credit access — meaning
it flows to farmers already close to the frontier. The strongest welfare
gains go to those who receive advice on new seed varieties and fertilisers
[Certified]. The service works. It reaches the wrong people.

**Nobody measured, so nothing improved.** Nigeria has run fertiliser subsidy
scheme after fertiliser subsidy scheme, repeatedly judged failures, partly
because **little empirical evidence was ever provided to guide the
decisions** [Certified — ScienceDirect]. Forty years of programmes, no
feedback loop.

## 3. Which horizontal failure explains each part

| Broken part | Horizontal failure | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Inputs leak into open market | Collection integrity, inverted — money going *out* untraceably | Ch. 1 |
| Help captured by the already-strong | No equity clause — the exact failure Ceará had to fix | Ch. 7 |
| Nothing measured, schemes repeat | No measurement, so no accountability | Ch. 4 |
| Programmes die between administrations | Flow without statutory housing | Rule 7, Ch. 2 |

**The convergence finding.** A sector economist, working independently of
this project, states it plainly: *Nigeria's agricultural productivity
deficit is not a shortage of proven technologies* — it stems from
overlapping institutional failures across seed supply, credit, insurance,
extension, market access, and land tenure [Certified — VoxDev, 2026].

That is Part II's first principle, reached from a completely different
starting point by someone who has never read this Blueprint. First
independent convergence on the vertical axis.

## 4. What is genuinely sector-specific

The residue the horizontals do not explain — and therefore the part worth
original work:

**Geography breaks national policy.** Georeferenced data show substantial
within-country variation in fertiliser transaction prices, driven by market
structure [Certified]. A single national fertiliser price or subsidy rate is
meaningless. This is a real sector constraint, not a governance failure.

**The technology already exists and is not being used.** There is a known
adoption frontier — proven seed varieties and practices that most Nigerian
farmers have not adopted. The gap is diffusion, not invention.

**Digital alone underperforms.** Digital advisory tools produce larger
productivity effects **when combined with in-person facilitation** than when
deployed alone, and tool design shapes who benefits [Certified — Fabregas et
al. 2025; Owusu et al. 2026]. This kills the cheap answer of solving
extension with an app.

## 5. What one state could try in 18 months

Powers required: state land and agriculture powers only. No federal
permission.

1. **Build a farmer registry.** Biometric or phone-verified, by ward. The
   sector literature names farmer identification systems as a missing
   foundation [Certified]. Nothing else on this list works without it.
2. **Replace physical allocation with traceable e-vouchers** redeemed at
   accredited dealers against the registry. Leakage becomes visible, then
   costly. This is Chapter 1's traceability principle pointed outward.
3. **Target extension at non-frontier farmers deliberately** — an explicit
   quota for farmers *without* existing assets or credit, since the default
   flow is toward those who already have them (Chapter 7's equity clause,
   transplanted).
4. **Pair digital advisory with human agents**, never digital alone.
5. **Publish a yield baseline** by ward before any of the above, and publish
   it annually after. This is the step forty years of Nigerian agriculture
   policy skipped.

**Kill condition.** If registered non-frontier households show no yield
movement by season three despite verified input delivery, the binding
constraint is not inputs — it is credit, land tenure, or market access.
Stop, and move the resources there. Say so publicly.
