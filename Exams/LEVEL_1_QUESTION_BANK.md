# Level 1 — Question Bank

*40 questions drawn from the 32 syllabus items. Every answer is in
[the book](../Book/00_THE_BOOK.md), and the chapter is named.*

**Pass mark: 50% — 20 of 40.** Fixed before anyone sits. Never adjusted
afterwards.

**How this bank is used.** A sitting draws 40 questions covering all 32
items. This document is public so that candidates study the *content*, not
the paper. Questions are refreshed each diet; the syllabus never changes
without notice.

---

## Section A — What is broken *(Chapters 1–9)*

**1.** Mama Ngozi pays four collectors in one week. Why can she be right that
government takes her money and does nothing, while the government is also
right that it has no money? *(Ch 1)*
> Because most of what she paid never reached government. The money left her
> hand and did not arrive. Both statements are true at once.

**2.** What single feature of the Monday collector's receipt book makes theft
untraceable? *(Ch 1)*
> It has no serial number. Nobody counts books issued against books returned,
> so nothing can be proved.

**3.** Enugu's revenue rose from ₦37.6bn to ₦180.5bn in one year. What did the
state actually change? *(Ch 1, 11)*
> It stopped collecting cash entirely — every ministry, every agency. Nobody's
> tax rate rose. You cannot steal what you cannot touch.

**4.** In real terms, how much did Enugu's revenue actually grow over two
years, and why is that different from the naira figure? *(Ch 1, 26)*
> About five times, not ten. Nigerian prices roughly doubled over the same
> period.

**5.** What two things does this book say about Enugu that weaken its own
argument? *(Ch 1)*
> Its debt more than doubled (₦82.5bn to ₦188.42bn), and it has not published
> audited accounts for 2024.

**6.** What share of Nigerian boreholes stop working within two years, and
why? *(Ch 2)*
> Four in ten. Nobody budgets to maintain them — there is money for building
> and none for the pump that fails later.

**7.** Name three things a primary health centre must have to count as
functioning. *(Ch 2, 15)*
> Any three of: open 24 hours, four skilled birth attendants, electricity,
> running water, drugs, a laboratory.

**8.** Nigeria promised 17,600 revitalised health centres in four years. How
many were delivered by September 2025, and who published that number?
*(Ch 2, 15)*
> 1,295 — 7.3%. The agency itself (NPHCDA) published the data; Nigeria Health
> Watch did the arithmetic.

**9.** How many Kaduna teachers failed a primary four level test in 2017, out
of how many? *(Ch 3)*
> 21,780 out of about 33,000 — roughly two in three.

**10.** Kaduna's governor won the strike, the court case, and sacked the
union president. Why did the teacher test still end? *(Ch 3, 6)*
> He left office in 2023 and it simply stopped. It lived in one man's will
> rather than in law, and had no constituency outside government.

**11.** In the same state and the same handover, what survived — and why?
*(Ch 3)*
> The contracts portal. It was written into law and civil society
> organisations were part of running it.

**12.** State the rule that follows from those two facts. *(Ch 3)*
> If it lives in one man, it dies with him. If it lives in law with somebody
> outside who would shout, it survives.

**13.** Name three of the five areas that failed the scorekeeper question, and
say what all five have in common. *(Ch 4)*
> Any three of: local government elections, land, electricity, security,
> leadership selection. In every one, the same body does the work and reports
> on the work.

**14.** Lagos gave the APC 375 of 376 council seats; Osun gave the PDP 332 of
332. Why does this book treat that as structural rather than partisan?
*(Ch 4)*
> Two opposite parties produced the same result in the same year. It is what
> happens when anyone marks his own exam.

**15.** What is the control case that proves the referee is the variable?
*(Ch 4)*
> INEC runs FCT council elections. In 2015 the opposition won two of three
> chairmanships.

**16.** State the two facts this book says are wrong with Nigeria. *(Ch 5)*
> Government does not need its citizens (71–92% of most states' money comes
> from Abuja), and nobody outside is counting whether it did anything.

**17.** Why does this book say the diagnosis is good news? *(Ch 5)*
> You cannot fix "Nigerians are corrupt" — it is an insult with no next step.
> You can change what a state lives on, and you can make somebody count.

**18.** What is the difference between a gateman and a gate? *(Ch 6)*
> A gateman works while he is there. A gate, a lock and a watching neighbour
> keep working when he travels.

**19.** Of roughly twelve failures documented in this book, how many were
externally caused? *(Ch 7)*
> About two.

**20.** What does blaming outsiders do that helps the wrong people? *(Ch 7)*
> It supplies an explanation that requires nobody to count anything — so it
> protects Nigerian officials, not foreign ones.

**21.** Name Nigeria's largest external creditor, and the most expensive
external debt. *(Ch 7)*
> The World Bank (~$19bn, cheap terms). Eurobonds — commercial paper Nigeria
> chose to issue at market rates.

**22.** Politicians versus debt service, as shares of the federal budget.
*(Ch 8)*
> National Assembly, Presidency and Judiciary together: 3.41%. Debt service:
> 28.52%.

**23.** What do the four state-startable chapters cost, and what is the
expensive part? *(Ch 8)*
> ₦1.1–4.1 billion — less than one flyover. The expensive part is on no
> invoice: giving the revenue service independence, and fighting people who
> live on the leak.

**24.** How do you know money is not the constraint? *(Ch 8)*
> The cheapest chapter — changing the sharing formula, which needs a lawyer
> and a signature — has failed five times since 2001.

**25.** Why do good people burn out in Nigerian government? *(Ch 9)*
> They are given the job without the tools, discover the problem is somebody's
> income, win a few fights, and then their term ends — and what they built
> cannot be inherited.

## Section B — What works *(Chapters 10–24)*

**26.** UBEC finishes 78% of its projects; the Ministry of Niger Delta Affairs
16%. Why does that kill both "it's culture" and "it's region"? *(Ch 10)*
> Culture is identical in both. And the widest gap in the country is between
> two offices in Abuja, not between north and south.

**27.** How many NDDC projects were abandoned, and at what stage? *(Ch 10)*
> Over 12,000 of 13,377 — abandoned *after* payment. Commission staff took the
> larger share of contractors' money.

**28.** State the one-sentence fix. *(Ch 10)*
> You cannot abandon a project after payment if payment only arrives after
> delivery.

**29.** Name the three jobs, and explain recurrent versus capital. *(Ch 11)*
> Block the leak, plan the money, show the work. Recurrent is the cost of
> having a government; capital is what is still standing in ten years.

**30.** Why is Kwara the proof rather than Enugu? *(Ch 11)*
> Its revenue service was created by law in 2015 and survived the 2019 "O to
> ge" handover to a different party. It was a gate, not a gateman.

**31.** Georgia raised police pay fifteenfold. Why was that not generosity?
*(Ch 12)*
> A man who cannot feed his family on his salary finds money elsewhere, and no
> test catches that afterwards.

**32.** What did the Civilian JTF supply that federal forces could not — and
what did it cost? *(Ch 13)*
> Knowing who people are; they could tell a neighbour from a stranger. Over
> 100 died, and the same researchers documented beatings, detentions and
> unaccountable local power.

**33.** Name the four steps of openness, and which one Nigeria has never
built. *(Ch 14)*
> Publish; make it usable; make it bind; measure what was saved. Nigeria has
> never measured what was saved.

**34.** Name the four constitutional provisions that block this book. *(Ch 16)*
> Sections 214–215 (no state police), 197 (state electoral commissions),
> 162(2) proviso (13% derivation), and the missing deadline in 162(2).

**35.** Why did the 2023 electricity amendment pass when the money formula
never has? *(Ch 16)*
> Electricity was a burden the centre was glad to shed. Money is not.

**36.** How much Nigerian property value is frozen, and why don't people
register? *(Ch 17)*
> About $300 billion — roughly 60% of annual national output. Registration can
> cost 15–30% of the property's value.

**37.** What did the water sector lose when three quarters of water points
became self-dug? *(Ch 18)*
> Its advocates. The people who could afford to exit were the ones whose
> complaints carried weight.

**38.** Why did Kiswahili work in Tanzania when Amharic failed in Ethiopia —
and what follows for Pidgin? *(Ch 19)*
> Kiswahili belonged to no dominant group; Amharic did. Pidgin belongs to no
> Nigerian ethnic group, so nobody submits by speaking it.

**39.** In Ceará, local governments that got more money did not spend it on
schools, yet learning improved. Why? *(Ch 20)*
> Mayors were being measured on something new, so they paid attention.
> Attention, not money, was the missing ingredient.

**40.** Why does the oil chapter come last in a book about what states can do?
*(Ch 23, 24)*
> It is the largest sector by money and the smallest by what a governor can
> change. Size is not leverage.

## Marking

One mark per question. **20 of 40 passes.**

Short-answer questions are marked on **substance, not wording.** A candidate
who states the mechanism correctly in Pidgin, or in their own words, is
correct. A candidate who reproduces our phrasing without the substance is
not.

**No question tests loyalty, biography, or opinion.**
