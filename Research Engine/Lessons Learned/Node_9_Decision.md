# Node 9 — Decision

**Node:** External constraint / neocolonialism
**Question investigated:** *Which of the failures documented in Nodes 1–8
and Sectors 01–11 are caused or sustained by external power — and where
does external power actually bind on a Nigerian state's ability to act?*

**Why this node exists.** The question was raised directly: can Nigeria
contend against Washington, London, Paris? It is the most emotionally loaded
question in the project and therefore the one where our method matters most.
The engine's rule applies without exception — evidence, mechanism, named
case, and a falsification attempt run against our own preferred answer.

**Status:** Decision-grade, with the honest note that this node has a
narrower evidence base than Nodes 1–8 and its most important claim is a
negative one.

---

## The decision

**External constraint is real, measurable, and concentrated in three
channels — debt structure, currency exposure, and verification dependence.
It does not explain the failures this project actually documented. Nigeria's
binding constraints are overwhelmingly domestic and mostly executable within
existing law. The strongest anti-colonial position available is capacity,
not grievance.**

**Confidence: ~70%** on the channel identification. **~85%** on the negative
claim (that documented failures are not externally caused), because that
rests on the whole corpus rather than on this node's searches.

## Part 1 — What external constraint measurably is

### Channel A: Debt structure — real, and the largest
- External debt stock **~$51.86bn** (Dec 2025), up 7% from $48.46bn in
  September [Certified — DMO].
- **World Bank Group is Nigeria's single largest external creditor** at
  **$18.3–19.9bn**. Multilateral loans total ~$23.19–23.85bn, about **45–46%
  of external debt** [Certified].
- Eurobonds: **$15–17.32bn**. Bilateral: ~$6–6.7bn, of which **China $5.16bn**
  and **France $609m** [Certified].
- 2025 external debt service: **~$5.15bn**, of which commercial ~$2.55bn
  (Eurobonds $2.49bn, 48.4% of the total), multilateral ~$1.996bn (IDA
  $769m, IMF $816m) [Certified].
- Debt service in the 2025 federal budget: **₦10.1tn, about 61% of projected
  federal revenue**; the IMF recorded interest-to-revenue above **80% in
  2023**, among the highest globally [Certified].

**Three findings that cut against the standard framing:**

1. **The largest single creditor is the World Bank, on concessional terms**,
   not a Western commercial predator. The most expensive debt is
   **Eurobonds — commercial paper Nigeria chose to issue**, at market rates,
   to investors who bought voluntarily. Nigeria returned to that market in
   late 2025 for $2.2bn.
2. **Domestic debt service now exceeds external.** Domestic was ~**54.5% of
   total debt servicing in 2025**, up from 45.8% in 2024 [Certified]. The
   biggest claim on Nigerian revenue is Nigerian creditors.
3. **China is not the dominant bilateral lender by the margin the discourse
   implies** ($5.16bn of ~$6bn bilateral, and repayments to Chinese
   creditors are *declining*), and France is under $610m.

**Honest counter-point, recorded:** concessionality is not the same as
neutrality. IDA lending carries policy dialogue, and multilateral leverage
is exercised through conditions and technical assistance rather than through
rates. That is real influence. But it is influence over *how* Nigeria
reforms, not a cause of ₦332bn sitting undrawn.

### Channel B: Currency and commodity exposure — real, and it hit this project directly
The 2023–25 devaluation and inflation cycle forced our own real-terms
restatement, which **retired Osun's growth claim and halved Enugu's**.
Naira depreciation raises the naira cost of every dollar obligation
mechanically. Oil price and volume are set outside Nigeria.

This is genuine external exposure. It is also **partly self-inflicted**:
the 2023 devaluation was a Nigerian policy decision, and the revenue
windfall states celebrated came from that decision plus subsidy removal.

### Channel C: Verification dependence — real, and the most under-discussed
The Absorption Playbook's central mechanism — pay on verified output — works
in Nigeria only inside donor programmes with **externally funded Independent
Verification Agents** (NSHIP, HOPE-GOV). We searched for a fully domestic
instance and did not find one.

**This is the sharpest form of dependence in the corpus, and nobody frames
it as neocolonialism** — because it looks like help. A country that cannot
fund a verifier willing to fail it is dependent in a way that no debt table
shows.

### Channel D: Historical — SAP, and the honest version
SAP (1986–94) imposed devaluation, privatisation, subsidy removal, social
spending cuts and public-sector rationalisation as conditions. Inflation
went from **5.4% (1986) to 40.9% (1989)**; manufacturing capacity
utilisation collapsed for want of foreign exchange for inputs [Certified].

**The literature is genuinely divided**, and we record both sides. Afrocentric
scholarship calls IMF conditionality "the crux impediment facing the
country." Other peer-reviewed work finds SAP "contributed positively to
economic growth" while conceding specific harms. And one uncomfortable
detail sits in the record: SAP was **presented as a homegrown programme
"produced by Nigerians for Nigerians"**, and the decision to accept it was
made by the Babangida regime, which used it to access foreign loans.

**Our reading:** the damage is real and the agency was shared. A framing that
removes Babangida from the story is not more anti-colonial; it is less
accurate.

## Part 2 — The falsification test: does external power explain what we found?

We ran every major documented failure against the question *"could
Washington, London or Paris have caused or prevented this?"*

| Documented failure | External cause? |
|---|---|
| ₦332bn of education money unaccessed; 27 of 36 states drew nothing (2024) | **No.** Nigerian money, Nigerian counterpart rules, Nigerian capacity |
| NDDC: 12,000 of 13,377 projects abandoned; staff took contractor payments | **No.** ₦10tn+ of Nigerian oil money, Nigerian commission |
| Lagos 375/376, Osun 332/332, Ondo 203/203 in LG elections | **No.** Nigerian commissions appointed by Nigerian governors |
| Revenue formula frozen since 1992; 2022 proposal never transmitted | **No.** A Nigerian president's desk |
| Land registration at 15–30% of property value; $300bn frozen | **No.** State fees set by state governments |
| Overhead +62.66% in a record revenue year | **No.** |
| 40% of boreholes dead within two years | **No.** Maintenance is unbudgeted |
| 16 states took electricity oversight and could not operate it | **No.** The devolution was Nigerian; the capacity gap was Nigerian |
| 2.1m hectares of farmland inaccessible; recruitment into banditry | **No** — though arms proliferation has cross-border and international dimensions we have not investigated |
| Debt service at 61% of federal revenue | **Partly yes** — but domestic debt is the larger share |
| Verification dependence | **Yes** |
| Naira/commodity exposure | **Yes, partly** |

**Result: of roughly a dozen documented failures, two are externally driven,
one is mixed, and the rest are domestic.**

**This is the node's central finding, and it is a negative one.** Not
"neocolonialism is a myth" — the channels above are real. But **the specific
failures this project spent twenty-three engine versions documenting are
almost entirely within Nigerian control.**

## Part 3 — The strategic argument, and why the framing matters

**The alibi problem.** Part II Layer 4b found that Nigeria's failures track
its missing scorekeepers. External-blame framing interacts with that in a
specific and damaging way: **it supplies a permanent explanation that
requires no scorekeeper.** If the boreholes failed because of Western
hegemony, nobody needs to publish the functionality register.

**Who the frame protects.** Not foreign actors — they are not seeking
Nigerian approval. It protects the commission officials who took contractor
payments, the governor whose commission won 375 of 376 seats, the ministry
that let ₦332bn sit idle. **Every alibi has a beneficiary, and in Nigeria's
case the beneficiaries are Nigerian.**

**This is the Medicine Rule at national scale.** Part I already says blame
builds nothing and *"leave James first, heal yourself."* Node 9 is that
argument with a table attached.

**The strongest anti-colonial position is the boring one.** A state that
collects its own revenue, executes its own projects, publishes its own
audited accounts and funds its own verifiers is structurally harder to
pressure than one that does not. Every channel identified above is *reduced
by domestic capacity*:
- Debt leverage falls when revenue rises (Chapter 1).
- Currency exposure falls when non-oil revenue rises (Chapter 1, Sector 07).
- Verification dependence ends when a state funds its own verifier
  (Absorption Playbook — and this is the single most direct
  sovereignty intervention in the corpus).

**Sovereignty is built by capacity, not asserted by grievance.**

## Part 4 — Where the argument against us is strongest

Recorded because this node is where our own bias is most likely to operate.

1. **Selection effect.** We investigated domestic governance for eight nodes
   and then asked whether the failures were foreign-caused. A project that
   had spent eight nodes on trade rules, currency architecture and
   commodity pricing would have found a different distribution. **Our
   evidence base was assembled to answer a different question.** This is a
   real limitation and it is why confidence is ~70% not higher.
2. **We did not investigate the historical causal chain.** Colonial
   extraction, the creation of a rentier state around export commodities,
   and the design of the 1999 Constitution under military rule with
   foreign-facing incentives — all plausibly shaped the *structures* we
   diagnose. Saying today's failure is domestic is not the same as saying
   the structure producing it was.
3. **Unexamined channels.** Illicit financial flows and transfer pricing;
   arms proliferation; Sahel security architecture and French basing;
   pharmaceutical and agricultural input dependence; credit rating agencies'
   effect on borrowing costs. Each could shift the balance and none was
   investigated.
4. **The strongest version of the opposing case:** external power operates
   less through visible coercion than by *shaping which options appear
   thinkable* — and a Blueprint that concludes "just build state capacity"
   may itself be an artefact of that shaping. We cannot falsify that from
   inside it. **Recorded as unfalsifiable-from-here, not as refuted.**

---

## Recommendation

1. **The Blueprint does not adopt an anti-imperial frame, and does not deny
   external constraint.** It states the three measurable channels, sizes
   them, and shows that each shrinks as domestic capacity grows.
2. **Add one line to the doctrine layer's candidate list**, tested like any
   other: *"Nobody is stopping us from counting our own money."*
3. **A sovereignty chapter is not needed.** Chapters 1, 4 and the Absorption
   Playbook already contain the whole sovereignty programme; what they
   lacked was the framing that says so.
4. **Fund your own verifier.** Of everything in this corpus, this is the
   most direct answer to Channel C, and no Nigerian government has done it.

## Owed
- Illicit financial flows / transfer pricing out of Nigeria — genuinely
  unexamined, plausibly large.
- The historical causal chain from colonial structure to the 1999
  Constitution.
- Whether any African state has successfully funded a domestic verification
  agent independent enough to fail its own government.
