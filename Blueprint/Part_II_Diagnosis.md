# Part II — The Diagnosis: What Is Actually Wrong

*Every politician names Nigeria's problems. This chapter is different in one
way only: every layer below cites evidence a skeptic can check, and every
claim carries its status. Where we are uncertain, it says so.*

---

## The method of this chapter

Most diagnoses stop at what hurts. This one descends four layers — from what
you can see, to what causes it, to what causes the causes, to the single
sentence underneath everything. The test of a good diagnosis is not that it
sounds deep; it is that the cure it implies actually worked somewhere. Each
layer below is anchored to the investigations (Nodes 1–6) published in this
repository's Research Engine.

## Layer 1 — What you can see

Corruption. Insecurity. Darkness where light should be. Poverty beside
plenty. Schools that don't teach, hospitals that don't heal, jobs that don't
exist. No Nigerian needs a citation for this layer; it is the weather.

## Layer 2 — What produces what you see

**A state that does not need its citizens.** Thirty of thirty-six states
depend on federally-shared oil money for 71–92% of their revenue [Certified,
Node 1]. A government funded without you has no structural reason to serve
you — and no structural fear of you.

**Institutions built around group shares, not competence.** The federal
character principle constitutionally encodes group allocation over merit in
public appointments [Certified, Node 2]; recruitment runs on quota and
patronage, and the literature records no state-level merit success until
Kaduna's contested experiment [Node 2].

**A security architecture that forbids the people closest to the problem.**
Sections 214–215 prohibit state police; the actors with local knowledge can
only "complement" [Certified, Node 3] — even though local knowledge is
precisely the capability federal forces lack [Certified: CJTF mechanism,
Node 3].

**Opacity as the default.** Only nine states pass an independent fiscal
transparency test; the causal chain from publishing to accountability is
broken at the usability and enforcement links even where publishing exists
[Certified, Node 4].

## Layer 3 — What produces the producers

**The constitutional gates are held by the winners.** The revenue formula
has been frozen since 1992 not for lack of review machinery, but because
every reform proposal must pass through the desk of the tier that loses —
and dies there. The 2022 proposal, nationally consulted, was simply never
transmitted [Certified, Node 5]. Reforms that shed federal burdens pass
(electricity, 2023); reforms that move federal money die [Certified, Node 5].

**Identity is activated, not ancient.** Ethnic identification in Nigeria
swings by twenty points within eighteen months, waxes at elections, and
strengthens when the state discriminates [Certified, Node 6]. Tribe is not
the disease; it is where people shelter when the state fails them — and a
weapon politicians reach for when competing over the central pot. Shrink
the pot's decisiveness and you shrink the weapon [Supported, Nodes 1/5/6].

**Nothing is built to survive its builder.** Reforms in Nigeria die not by
defeat but by succession: the organized loser waits out the founder
[Certified, Node 2 — Kaduna's competency testing]. What survives is what is
housed in statute with a constituency [Certified, Nodes 2/4/1 — Kaduna's
OGP machinery; Kwara's revenue authority through a hostile succession].

## Layer 4 — The first principle

> **The Nigerian state lacks the capacity and the incentives to consistently
> act in the long-term interests of its citizens — because it is funded
> without them, staffed by quota, closed to their knowledge, opaque to their
> eyes, and constitutionally gated by the beneficiaries of all four.**

Everything in Layer 1 is a consequence of this sentence. Corruption is not
the root; it is what a low-capacity, misaligned-incentive state *does*.
Tribe and religion are not the root; they are where citizens flee and what
elites weaponize. Oil is not the root; countries with identical resources
diverged — the incentive structure, not the resource, is the engine
[Certified: the Layer 2–3 evidence above].

## Why this diagnosis carries hope, not despair

Because every mechanism named above has been **beaten somewhere — including
inside Nigeria**:

- A structurally ordinary state escaped revenue dependence [Enugu — Certified,
  Node 1], and another built the machinery by statute and carried it through
  a hostile succession [Kwara — Certified, Node 1].
- Merit testing was executed and sustained for five years against organized
  resistance [Kaduna — Certified, Node 2].
- Community knowledge measurably beat an insurgency where federal force
  alone could not [CJTF — Certified, Node 3].
- A Nigerian state holds a perfect independent transparency score
  [Kaduna — Certified, Node 4].
- The Constitution itself has been amended toward devolution, peacefully
  [electricity, 2023 — Certified, Node 5].
- Nigeria's own fifty-year-old contact machine measurably reduces ethnic
  bias, with effects lasting years [NYSC — Certified (causal), Node 6].

The diagnosis is severe. It is also, demonstrably, not terminal. Complex is
not impossible — and nothing above waits for a hero. Every proof named is a
machine, and machines can be copied, housed in law, and defended.

## What the diagnosis prescribes (the bridge to Part IV)

If the first principle is right, the cure is not better people but changed
structure, in this order: fund the state from its people's work (Chapter 1);
select and keep the competent by law, not by oga (Chapter 2); open the
security architecture to local knowledge under law (Chapter 3); make every
naira traceable and every trace usable (Chapter 4); take the open
constitutional doors now and build coalitions for the locked ones
(Chapter 5); and strengthen the machines that already make Nigerians out of
Nigerians (Chapter 6). Each chapter runs the same eleven-field discipline,
and each answers to the same standard: **not achieved — irreversible.**

### Convergence note (added with Sector Brief 01)

The vertical axis has begun testing this diagnosis from outside. Working
independently of this project, a sector economist concludes that Nigeria's
agricultural productivity deficit is **not a shortage of proven
technologies** but stems from overlapping institutional failures across
seed supply, credit, insurance, extension, market access and land tenure
[Certified — VoxDev, 2026].

That is Layer 4, reached from agriculture rather than from governance, by
someone who has never read this Blueprint. Each sector brief that
decomposes into the same root causes strengthens the diagnosis by
convergence; any sector that fails for reasons the horizontals cannot
explain will force Layer 4 to be revised. Both outcomes are recorded.

Convergences so far: **6 of 8 planned sectors** (Agriculture, Power,
Primary Health, Basic Education, Water & Sanitation, Transport & Capital
Projects).

Power converged differently and more usefully: it did not merely restate the
diagnosis — it **corrected a prescription of ours** (Mistakes 8.1). A
vertical that can amend the horizontal is doing the work it was designed
for.

Primary health converged a third way: it **demonstrated the cure**. NPHCDA
published its monitoring dashboard in August 2025; an independent watchdog
promptly used that data to show only 7.3% of the four-year target had been
met. Chapter 4's chain — publication → usability → scrutiny — is already
running in this sector, against the publishing agency's own interest. The
Blueprint borrows from health here rather than instructing it.

### Layer 3 addition — absorptive capacity (earned by the vertical axis)

Three sectors produced the same failure independently, across three
different kinds of resource:

- **Authority:** 16 states took electricity regulatory oversight by early
  2026 and several could not operate it — non-functional regulators,
  licences issued outside the legal framework, no measurable supply
  improvement [Sector 02].
- **Money:** ₦332bn of basic-education funding sits unaccessed or unspent;
  27 of 36 states accessed nothing in 2024 [Sector 04].
- **Infrastructure:** public water utilities produce in 28 states but
  function in only 16, and 40% of boreholes fail within two years
  [Sector 05].

**The finding:** *Nigeria's constraint is not only that the state lacks
resources or authority — it is that the state frequently cannot absorb,
operate, or maintain the resources and authority it already holds.*

This matters because it changes what reform means. Giving a low-absorption
state more money, or more powers, does not produce more delivery; it
produces idle accounts, dormant regulators, and decaying assets — and
sometimes a worse outcome than before the transfer (Sector 02). Capacity is
not a precondition to be assumed. It is the reform.

Earned under Law Zero by three independent sector cases; promoted from
candidate at Sector Brief 05.

**Refinement (Sector Brief 06): absorption is a variable, not a national
condition.** Independent tracking of 2,760 federal projects across 30 states
found **97.5% of abandoned-but-paid-for projects concentrated in five
states** — Taraba, Abia, Nasarawa, Adamawa, Ogun [Certified — Tracka].

**Correction to our own earlier phrasing.** An earlier draft of this section
described "seventeen states with none." That was too clean. The fuller
Tracka data shows: **Cross River alone recorded no abandoned, unexecuted or
fraudulently delivered projects**; top-ten completion rates run only ~52–63%
(Katsina and Rivers leading, Enugu 8th at 61.96%, Kaduna 10th at 59%); and
*non-execution* concentrates in a **different** five states — Benue 40%,
Ondo 32.4%, Kwara 30.4%, Akwa Ibom 27.3%, Sokoto 25.6%. Nationally, of the
2024 capital budget tracked, **52.10% completed, 23.91% ongoing, and ~24%
abandoned, not executed, or fraudulently delivered despite funds released**.
The honest claim is narrower: abandonment-after-payment is concentrated;
non-execution is concentrated differently; and even the best performers
complete only about six projects in ten.
Capital-budget execution shows the same failure at federal level (17.66% of
the capital budget spent to Q3 2025 on the Budget Office's own figures,
disputed by the Finance Ministry at 76–84% on a wider measure), but the
state distribution is the more actionable fact.

**And the sharper cut: absorption is organisational, not geographic.**
The same tracking ranks federal agencies as well as states.
**UBEC completed 78% of its projects; the Federal Ministry of Agriculture
68%; the Rural Electrification Agency 65% — against the Lower Benue River
Basin Development Authority at 20% and the Ministry of Niger Delta Affairs
at 16%** [Certified — Tracka]. Same country, same contractors, same
procurement law, same year: a **62-point spread between agencies**.

That finding kills the two laziest explanations at once. It is not culture,
because the culture is constant across agencies. It is not region, because
the spread is widest *within* the federal government. Some organisations
execute and others do not, and the difference is inside them.

Note the pairing this produces: UBEC is the federation's best-executing
agency **and** the agency whose money 27 of 36 states could not draw down in
2024 [Sector 04]. UBEC can build. The states cannot absorb what UBEC funds.
Capacity failure is specific to organisations, not general to the country.

**Mechanism candidate — consequence, not competence.** Tracka's own reading:
*"Because there are no consequences for abandonment of a project, it is
almost a tradition for local officials to just decide to start another
project even when there are several other ones that need to be executed."*
If that is right, absorption is an incentive problem before it is a training
problem, and the fix is enforcement — which routes back to Chapter 4.
**Owed:** what UBEC does that the low performers do not. Named in the
worktree as the most valuable unrun investigation in this repository.
