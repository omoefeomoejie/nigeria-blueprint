# What Government Costs Before It Does Anything

*Completing the finding that surfaced while costing the chapters: nobody
publishes what reform costs, but everybody publishes what government costs.
The second number turns out to explain a lot.*

---

## 1. What the sector produces now

**Subnational spending, 2024:** the 36 states collectively spent **₦15.63
trillion**, up 64.69% from ₦9.49tn in 2023. Lagos alone accounted for
₦2.37tn, about 14.95% of all state spending [Certified — BudgIT State of
States].

Disaggregated:
- **Personnel:** ₦1.85tn → **₦2.28tn** (+23.24%); three states cut theirs.
- **Overhead:** ₦1.5tn → **₦2.44tn** (**+62.66%**), Lagos leading, with
  Abia, Bayelsa, Imo and Ondo reducing.
- **Capital:** ₦4.06tn → **₦7.63tn** (+87.93%), every state up except Ondo.

**And a genuine turning point:** in 2024, capital expenditure **exceeded**
recurrent by about ₦1.26tn, reversing 2023 when operating costs exceeded
capex by ₦600bn [Certified].

At federal level the picture is worse and clearer: **debt service alone
takes ₦8.27tn, 28.52% of the budget** — while the National Assembly,
Presidency and Judiciary together account for ~₦990bn, or 3.41%
[Certified].

## 2. What is broken

**Overhead grew almost three times faster than personnel.** +62.66% against
+23.24%. Personnel growth has an explanation — a new minimum wage.
Overhead — travel, vehicles, "ancillary expenses" — has none, and it grew
fastest in the same year states celebrated record revenue.

**The political-perks framing is popular and mostly wrong.** Legislature,
Presidency and Judiciary are 3.41% of the federal budget. Debt service is
28.52%. **Cutting every politician's salary to zero would fund about six
weeks of debt service.** The cost-of-governance debate as conducted in
Nigerian public argument is aimed at the visible 3% and not the structural
28%.

*(This matters for Part I's Medicine Rule: the popular villain is the wrong
villain, and a movement that chases him wastes its energy.)*

**Debt service is the real crowd-out.** Nearly a third of the federal budget
is spent before a road is built or a teacher is paid [Certified].

**Nobody publishes what reform costs.** Every state publishes personnel,
overhead and capital. **No state publishes what any specific reform cost or
what it returned.** So the price of governance can be compared across
states; the price of *good* governance cannot.

## 3. Which horizontal failure explains each part

| Broken part | Horizontal failure | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Overhead +62.66% in a revenue boom | Collection improved, spending discipline did not | Ch. 1 layer 2 |
| Debt service crowding out delivery | Borrowing ahead of absorption capacity | Sector 06, Part II L3 |
| Reform costs unpublished | Missing scorekeeper | Part II Layer 4b |
| Public debate aimed at 3% not 28% | No usable public data → wrong target | Ch. 4 links 2–3 |

**The completed finding.** Part II Layer 4b says Nigeria's failures track its
missing scorekeepers. This is a clean instance with an unusual shape: the
inputs are scored (BudgIT publishes personnel, overhead and capex for all 36
states, annually, comparably) and **the returns are not** (no state
publishes what a reform cost or produced).

> **Nigeria can rank its states by what government costs. It cannot rank
> them by what governing achieves per naira.**

That is why the national argument is about *size* of government rather than
*productivity* of government — the only number anyone has is the input one.
And it is why BudgIT's own methodology note admits its ranking "equates
higher capital spending with progress" while ignoring efficiency, impact and
service delivery [Certified — BudgIT's own statement of its limitations].

Even our best scorekeeper is scoring the input.

## 4. What is genuinely sector-specific

**The 2024 capex reversal may be the most important unexamined fact in
Nigerian public finance.** Capital exceeded recurrent for the first time in
the series. Either states genuinely turned a corner, or the revenue windfall
from subsidy removal and devaluation flowed into capital budgets that then
went unexecuted — which is exactly what Sector 06 found (17.66% capital
implementation to Q3 2025 on the Budget Office's own figures). **Both
stories fit the same number.** Nobody has separated them. *Owed, and it is
a large question.*

**Overhead is the softest target in Nigerian public finance and nobody aims
at it.** It grew 62.66%, four states cut it, and no state publishes a
line-item breakdown. A state that published its overhead by department would
be the first.

**"Cost of governance" as a phrase has been captured.** It is used to mean
politicians' salaries, which are 3.41%. The structural items — debt service,
overhead growth, unexecuted capital — are less quotable and vastly larger.

## 5. What one state could try in 18 months

1. **Publish overhead by department, quarterly.** Nobody does this. It is
   free, and it makes the fastest-growing line visible.
2. **Publish cost-and-return for one reform.** What the revenue platform
   cost, what it collected. One honest pair of numbers, and the state
   becomes the only one in the federation that can prove it governs
   efficiently rather than merely cheaply.
3. **Report capital *executed*, not capital budgeted** — the gap between
   them is where the 2024 reversal will be tested.
4. **Set an overhead ceiling as a share of recurrent** and publish
   performance against it.

**Kill condition.** If overhead publication produces no reduction within
two budget cycles, transparency alone is insufficient here and the state
should move to a hard statutory ceiling (Chapter 4's enforcement link).
