# Oil & Gas

*This is the sector everyone expects a Nigerian blueprint to lead with. We
put it last, and the reason is the finding. Here is the largest, most
reliable, most automatic money in the federation, producing the worst
delivery record in the country. Money was never the constraint. This sector
is the proof.*

---

## 1. What the sector produces now

Oil-producing states shared **₦1.51 trillion** through the 13% derivation in
2025, more than double the ₦671.9bn of 2024 [Certified — NBS/FAAC via
BudgIT].

In the first quarter of 2026 alone, **₦321.9bn** went to eleven states.
**Delta took ₦101.6bn, Bayelsa ₦71.6bn, Akwa Ibom ₦69.4bn** — about 75%
between three states. Add Rivers at ₦46.1bn and four states hold nearly 90%
of the pool [Certified].

The scale of the gap: Delta's quarterly allocation is roughly **2.16 million
times** what Enugu or Kogi received in the same period, since new
oil-producing states earn only symbolic amounts [Certified].

This money requires no collection effort, no tax base, no citizen
compliance, and no administrative capacity. It arrives.

## 2. What is broken

**The outcome test fails on the sector's own terms.** Over ₦10 trillion has
passed through the 13% derivation, and oil-producing communities remain
poor [Certified]. In 2026, host community representatives publicly accused
state governments of politicising the fund, arguing that oil-bearing
communities "have not received commensurate benefits from the resources
extracted from their lands" [Certified].

**The region holds the country's worst delivery record.** The forensic audit
of the Niger Delta Development Commission found **over 12,000 of 13,377
projects abandoned** after payment, with commission personnel taking the
larger share of contractor payments [Certified — see the Absorption
Playbook]. The Ministry of Niger Delta Affairs ranks last of all federal
agencies on project completion, at 16%.

Put those two facts together. The most money and the least delivery, in the
same place, at the same time.

**Concentration inside concentration.** Four states hold 90% of the
derivation pool, and within those states the money stops at the government
rather than reaching the communities whose land is being drilled.

## 3. Which horizontal failure explains each part

| Broken part | Horizontal failure | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Vast revenue, minimal delivery | Execution/conversion, at maximum scale | Ch. 1 |
| Money captured before it reaches communities | Payment chain integrity | Absorption Playbook |
| No consequence for abandonment | Enforcement absent | Ch. 4 |
| A government funded without its citizens | The rentier mechanism itself | Part II, Layer 4 |

**Ninth convergence — and the purest one.** Every other sector shows a state
failing to convert revenue into delivery. This one removes every excuse. No
collection problem, because the money is automatic. No tax base problem,
because no citizen pays it. No absorption-of-authority problem, because the
authority is constitutional. And the delivery record is the worst in the
federation.

> **Part II's first principle, tested at the extreme: give a state money
> without making it depend on its citizens, and delivery does not follow.
> It gets worse.**

## 4. What is genuinely sector-specific

**A state cannot move this lever.** Derivation is set by production volume
under Section 162(2). No governor can raise it, cut it, or redirect it. The
only variable is extraction the state does not control. This is why the
brief comes last: it is the largest sector by value and the smallest by
state-level actionability.

**The revenue is volatile and shrinking in real terms as a share of
purpose.** Derivation doubled in a year on price and exchange-rate movement,
not on any decision made in Nigeria. A budget built on it is a budget built
on other people's weather.

**One real mechanism exists, and it bypasses the state.** Under the
Petroleum Industry Act, **155 Host Community Development Trusts** have been
incorporated, with statutory spending limits: **75% to projects, 20% to
investment, 5% to administration** [Certified — NUPRC]. That is a flow
housed in statute, with a fixed allocation rule, delivering to communities
without passing through a state government.

Compare it to the NDDC, which had none of those constraints and abandoned
90% of its projects. The HCDT design is what the region has needed for
twenty years. **Whether it works is unknown, and it is the single most
important thing to watch in this sector.**

## 5. What one state could try in 18 months

Honest answer: **very little, and that is the point.**

1. **Publish derivation receipts and spending, line by line.** No state does
   this. It is the only meaningful lever a governor holds here, and it costs
   nothing.
2. **Route a defined share to the Host Community Development Trusts**, as
   host communities have formally requested. The trusts already exist with
   statutory spending rules; a state that funds them is buying delivery
   discipline it does not have to build.
3. **Resolve abandoned projects before awarding new ones** — the region
   holds the country's largest stock of abandoned work.
4. **Treat derivation as temporary.** Any state whose plan depends on it
   past 2040 is planning on someone else's weather. Chapter 1 applies here
   more than anywhere: build the tax state while the oil money is still
   arriving.

**Kill condition.** If HCDT-routed funds show the same abandonment pattern
as NDDC within three years, statutory spending rules are insufficient
without independent verification, and the sector requires the payment-chain
fix from the Absorption Playbook rather than better allocation rules.
