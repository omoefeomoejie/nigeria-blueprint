# Chapter 7 — Learning: Paying for Results, Not Promises

*The test is simple and brutal: can a Nigerian child born today read, count,
and compete? Not "is she enrolled." Enrolment is a building. Learning is the
point.*

---

## 1. Desired Outcome
By year 10, a randomly selected Primary 6 pupil in the adopting state reads
and does arithmetic at grade level, measured by an independent assessment
published annually — with the gap between best and worst local governments
narrowing, not widening.

## 2. Structural Matches
- **Ceará, Brazil** [Certified — causal]: the fifth-poorest of 26 states;
  largest national IDEB growth since 2005; ten municipalities in the
  national top 20. Border-discontinuity evidence: **+0.15 SD** in maths and
  language for exposed ninth-graders, **doubling** where technical
  assistance was supplied.
- **Vietnam** [Confounded]: a genuine outlier — poorest PISA participant,
  outperforming far richer countries even after correcting for out-of-school
  bias — but at most ~30% of its performance is explained by child,
  household and school variables, and most of that is *household* input.
  Cited here as proof that poverty is not destiny; **not** as a model.

## 3. The Mechanism

Tie the money to whether children learn, then let the local government spend
it on whatever it likes.

Ceará put 18% of its transfer quota behind a simple index: can a child read
by primary two, and do arithmetic by primary five. Because the reward is
general money rather than education money, everyone in the local government
cares. The chairman cares. The finance officer cares. Not just the education
desk, which nobody listens to anyway.

**The counterintuitive core [Certified]:** municipalities receiving more
money did **not** spend more on education (~₦0.45 of every naira-equivalent).
The learning gains came from *attention and accountability*, not from
budget. This chapter therefore does not ask for more education spending. It
asks for a different incentive.

## 4. Preconditions (Rule 5)
Two, stated by the source: **a decentralised school system** — Nigeria has
this; basic education already sits with states and local governments — and
**a robust monitoring system** — Nigeria lacks this, so building the
assessment is step one, not step three.

## 5. Nigerian Constraints
No credible state-level learning baseline exists in most states; assessment
capacity is thin; and any index invites gaming (teaching to the test,
excluding weak pupils from sitting). Design must assume gaming, not hope
against it.

## 6. Nigerian Adaptation
A willing governor, **using powers he already has** — no amendment, no
federal permission:
- Build the assessment first: an independent annual test of P2 literacy and
  P5 numeracy/reading across all LGs, published in full.
- Assign a defined share of the state's discretionary transfers to an
  Education Quality Index built on it.
- Keep the reward **general-purpose** so the whole LG government is engaged.
- Supply **technical assistance** — pedagogical and managerial — because
  the evidence says this doubles the effect.
- **Equity clause from day one:** penalise concentrations of low performers.
  Without it, gains land in the strongest schools [Certified].
- Anti-gaming: assessment run independently, sitting rates published
  alongside scores, sanction for exclusion.

## 7. Political Economy
Losers: LGs currently receiving transfers regardless of performance, and
anyone whose position depends on unmeasured outcomes. But note the design's
gift — no one loses *money already held*; the reform changes the formula
for a discretionary slice going forward, which is politically far cheaper
than a dismissal-based reform (Chapter 2's lesson). Teachers are not the
target here; mayors are.

## 8. Early-Win Pilot
18 months: independent baseline assessment completed and published for
every LG in the state (the first such public number in most states);
the index formula gazetted; first transfer cycle computed under it;
technical assistance teams deployed to the bottom quartile.

## 9. Scale Strategy
Publish the LG league table and the state's own results annually.
Ceará's model has a World Bank implementation guide written for exactly
this replication — adoption cost is low and the evidence travels with it.

## 10. Success Metrics
Year 1: baseline published; formula gazetted. Year 3: ≥0.15 SD improvement
against baseline in the exposed cohort, with bottom-quartile LGs improving
at least as fast as the top (the equity test). Year 10: Outcome (§1) met.

## 11. Kill Condition
If by year 3 scores rise only in already-strong LGs, or sitting rates fall
(pupils excluded to protect scores), the mechanism is producing inequality
or fraud — halt transfers under the index, fix the rule (Ceará's own
low-performer penalty is the tested fix), and re-baseline.

## Irreversibility Test (Rule 7)
**Stock:** the assessment instrument, the trained assessors, the published
baseline series — once a state knows its numbers, unknowing them is costly.
**Flow:** the index-linked transfer formula itself, which must sit in a
gazetted regulation or state law with the league table published annually
and civil society as its constituency. A formula that lives in a governor's
circular dies with the governor [Rule 7; Node 2's warning].

### Correction (from Sector Brief 04)
This chapter assumed the binding Nigerian constraint was **measurement**.
Basic-education data says it is at least equally **absorption**: ₦332bn in
federal education money sits unaccessed or unspent because drawing it
requires 50% counterpart funding and project-preparation capacity states
often lack; 27 of 36 states accessed nothing in 2024 [Certified — UBEC,
Plan International]. **You cannot tie a transfer to outcomes if the
recipient cannot process a transfer.** Sequence corrected to:
**absorption → measurement → incentive.** Technical assistance is not the
impact-doubler here; it is the entry condition.

Also: the measurement precondition may now be met. UBEC reports a completed
**National Learning Assessment** with the Federal Ministry of Education
[Certified]. **Owed:** verify its credibility and state-level granularity —
if it holds, §6's baseline step is already built.

---

### Scorekeeper constraint

This chapter's mechanism depends on an assessment that determines how much
money local governments receive. **That assessment must never be run by the
state whose transfers depend on it.**

If a state ever both administers the test and pays out against it, the
mechanism inverts into the failure documented in Chapter 8, where the body
being measured controls the measurement. Ceará's design assumed independent
assessment; Nigeria's National Learning Assessment is federally run, which
satisfies the condition today. It is a condition, not a detail.

---

### Cost
**₦500m–₦2bn, and this is the genuinely expensive chapter — because the assessment recurs annually or the mechanism dies.** Instrument design, enumerator training, fieldwork across every LG, marking, publication. Every year.

Two things reduce it. The **National Learning Assessment already exists federally** [Certified — UBEC]; if it is credible and state-disaggregated, a state's cost collapses from administration to verification. And **the transfer money is not new spending** — it is a reallocation of transfers the state already sends.

This is the least confident figure in `COSTING.md`, and it is flagged there as such.
