# What Is Actually Wrong

*Every politician can name Nigeria's problems. This chapter is different in
one way only. Every claim below cites something you can go and check, and
where we are unsure, it says so.*

---

## How to read this

Most diagnoses stop at what hurts. This one goes down four layers: what you
can see, what causes it, what causes the causes, and the one sentence
underneath all of it.

The test of a diagnosis is not whether it sounds deep. It is whether the
cure it implies has worked somewhere. Each layer below points to the
investigations published in this repository.

## Layer 1 — What you can see

Corruption. Insecurity. Darkness where light should be. Poverty beside
plenty. Schools that do not teach, clinics that do not heal, jobs that do
not exist. Nobody needs a footnote for this layer. It is the weather.

## Layer 2 — What produces what you see

**A state that does not need you.** Thirty of thirty-six states get between
71 and 92 per cent of their money from oil shared in Abuja [Certified,
Node 1]. A government funded whether or not you work has no reason to care
whether you work.

**Offices shared out by group, not filled by ability.** The federal
character principle writes group allocation into the Constitution for public
appointments [Certified, Node 2]. Recruitment runs on quota and connection.
No Nigerian state had tried merit selection at scale until Kaduna, and that
attempt is contested.

**A security system that shuts out the people who know the ground.**
Sections 214 and 215 forbid state police, so the people with local knowledge
can only assist [Certified, Node 3]. Yet local knowledge is exactly what
federal forces lack, and exactly what beat Boko Haram in Maiduguri.

**Opacity by default.** Only nine states pass an independent fiscal
transparency test, and even where publishing happens the chain breaks before
anything binds [Certified, Node 4].

## Layer 3 — What produces the producers

**The gates are held by the winners.** The formula for sharing Nigeria's
money has not moved since 1992, not because nobody proposed a change, but
because every proposal must pass the desk of the tier that loses. A full
proposal reached the President in 2022 and was never sent forward
[Certified, Node 5]. Reforms that hand the centre a headache pass;
electricity moved to the states in 2023. Reforms that move the centre's
money do not.

**Identity is switched on, not inherited.** How Nigerians describe
themselves swings twenty points in eighteen months, rises at election time,
and hardens when government treats a group unfairly [Certified, Node 6].
Tribe is where people shelter when the state fails, and what politicians
reach for when they are fighting over one central pot.

**Nothing is built to outlast its builder.** Reforms here rarely lose. They
get outwaited. Kaduna's teacher testing survived a strike and a court case,
then stopped the month the governor left [Certified, Node 2]. What survives
is what sits in law with people outside government defending it — Kaduna's
open government machinery, Kwara's revenue service through a hostile
handover [Certified, Nodes 1, 4].

**The state cannot always use what it already has.** Sixteen states took
control of electricity and several could not run it. ₦332 billion of
education money sits undrawn while twenty million children are out of
school. Water utilities produce in twenty-eight states and work in sixteen,
and four in ten boreholes die within two years [Certified, Sectors 02, 04,
05].

## Layer 4 — The one sentence

> **The Nigerian state lacks the capacity and the incentives to act
> consistently in the long-term interests of its citizens. It is funded
> without them, staffed by quota, closed to their knowledge, opaque to their
> eyes, and the gates that could change any of this are held by the people
> who benefit from all of it.**

Everything in Layer 1 follows from that. Corruption is not the root, it is
what such a state does. Tribe and religion are not the root, they are where
citizens flee and what elites pick up. Oil is not the root either. Norway
and Botswana had the same resource and went elsewhere. The incentives are
the engine, not the oil.

### Layer 3 addition — the state often cannot absorb what it holds

Three sectors produced the same failure independently, across three
different kinds of resource:

- **Authority.** Sixteen states took electricity regulatory oversight by
  early 2026 and several could not operate it: regulators that existed on
  paper, licences issued outside the legal framework, no measurable
  improvement in supply [Sector 02].
- **Money.** ₦332bn of basic-education funding unaccessed or unspent;
  twenty-seven of thirty-six states accessed nothing in 2024 [Sector 04].
- **Infrastructure.** Public water utilities produce in twenty-eight states
  and function in sixteen; 40% of boreholes fail within two years
  [Sector 05].

**The finding:** *the state frequently cannot absorb, operate or maintain
the resources and authority it already holds.*

This changes what reform means. Give a low-absorption state more money or
more powers and you do not get more delivery. You get idle accounts, dormant
regulators, decaying assets, and sometimes a worse result than before the
transfer. Capacity is not a precondition to assume. It is the reform.

Earned under Law Zero by three independent sector cases.

**And it is a variable, not a national condition.** Independent tracking of
2,760 federal projects found 97.5% of abandoned-but-paid-for projects in
five states — Taraba, Abia, Nasarawa, Adamawa, Ogun [Certified — Tracka].

*(Corrected: an earlier draft said "seventeen states with none." Only Cross
River recorded no abandoned, unexecuted or fraudulently delivered projects;
top-ten completion rates run about 52–63%; non-execution concentrates in a
different five states. The narrower claim is the accurate one.)*

**The sharper cut is organisational, not geographic.** The same tracking
ranks agencies: UBEC completed 78% of its projects, the Federal Ministry of
Agriculture 68%, the Rural Electrification Agency 65%, against the Lower
Benue River Basin Development Authority at 20% and the Ministry of Niger
Delta Affairs at 16% [Certified]. Same country, same contractors, same law,
same year. Sixty-two points apart.

That kills the two laziest explanations at once. Not culture, because
culture is constant across those five rows. Not region, because the spread
is widest inside one government. Some organisations execute and some do not,
and the difference sits inside them.

One pairing makes the point. UBEC is the federation's best executor **and**
the agency whose money twenty-seven states could not draw down. UBEC can
build. The states cannot absorb what UBEC funds.

**Why:** at the failed end, an independent forensic audit of 13,777 projects
found commission personnel taking the larger share of contractor payments,
with contractors then abandoning the work. At the working end, funds are
released only after delivery is verified. The difference is when the money
moves, not who the people are. See the Absorption Playbook.

## Why this is not a counsel of despair

Every mechanism named above has been beaten somewhere, and four of them
inside Nigeria:

- An ordinary state escaped revenue dependence, and another built the
  machinery in law and carried it through a hostile handover [Enugu, Kwara —
  Node 1].
- Merit testing was executed at scale against a strike and a lawsuit
  [Kaduna — Node 2].
- Community knowledge beat an insurgency that federal force alone could not
  [Borno — Node 3].
- A Nigerian state holds a perfect independent transparency score [Kaduna —
  Node 4].
- The Constitution was amended toward devolution, peacefully [electricity,
  2023 — Node 5].
- Nigeria's own youth service scheme measurably reduces ethnic prejudice,
  and the effect lasts years [NYSC — Node 6].

The diagnosis is severe. It is not terminal. And nothing on that list waited
for a hero. Every one is a machine, and machines can be copied, written into
law, and defended.

## What follows from this

If the sentence in Layer 4 is right, the cure is not better people but
changed structure, in this order: fund the state from its people's work
(Chapter 1); pick and keep the competent by law rather than by favour
(Chapter 2); open security to local knowledge under law (Chapter 3); make
every naira traceable and every trace usable (Chapter 4); take the open
constitutional doors now and build for the locked ones (Chapter 5);
strengthen what already makes Nigerians out of Nigerians (Chapter 6); pay
for learning rather than for buildings (Chapter 7); and close the window
between the vote counted and the number announced (Chapter 8).

Each chapter runs the same discipline, and each answers to the same
standard. Not achieved. Irreversible.

### Convergence note

Six sector briefs tested this diagnosis from outside. All six decomposed
into the failures named above, and the three sharpest confirmations came
from people with no relationship to this project: a sector economist
("Nigeria's agricultural productivity deficit is not a shortage of proven
technologies"), BudgIT's Tracka after visiting 2,760 projects ("this is no
longer a funding problem"), and UBEC's Executive Secretary ("not the amount
of money accessed or spent, but the impact").

Convergences: **8 of 9 planned sectors.** Three did more than confirm. Power corrected one of our prescriptions;
primary health showed the cure already running, with an agency publishing
data a watchdog immediately used against it; and housing inverted the
diagnosis — the only sector where the state is not failing to deliver
something but preventing citizens from using what they already own. $300bn
of Nigerian property, roughly 60% of GDP, is frozen because registering it
costs up to a third of its value.
