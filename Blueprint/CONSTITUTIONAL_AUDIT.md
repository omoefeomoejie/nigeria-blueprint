# What Needs the Constitution Changed, and What Does Not

*A reader should be able to tell in thirty seconds which parts of this book
a governor could start on Monday and which require a fight in Abuja. Until
now they could not. This fixes that.*

*Method: every chapter and sector run through Node 5's gate map — who holds
the gate, and what do they lose? Node 5 established that burden-shedding
reforms pass and money-moving reforms die at the gate held by the loser.*

## Chapter by chapter

### Chapter 1 — Revenue: CLEAR
Nothing in the Constitution prevents a state from ending cash collection,
digitising its revenue service, harmonising its taxes, or publishing a
conversion ledger. States have residual competence over their own revenue
administration, and the working cases (Enugu, Kwara, Osun) all operated
within existing law.

**The one constitutional shadow:** the *ceiling* on how much autonomy this
buys is set by s.162's allocation formula, which a state cannot touch. A
state can reduce its dependence; it cannot change the terms of the
dependence. Chapter 1 raises the floor. Chapter 5 is about the ceiling.

### Chapter 2 — Leadership selection: CLEAR, WITH A SHADOW
Kaduna executed mass competency testing and recruitment entirely within
existing law, survived litigation, and dismissed the union's national
president. So the power exists and has been tested in court.

**The shadow is the federal character principle** (s.14(3) and the Federal
Character Commission), which requires that appointments reflect federal
character to avoid domination by persons from a few states or groups. At
*state* level, its equivalents govern LGA-balance in state appointments.

**Honest reading, against our own bias:** we have repeatedly called federal
character an obstacle to merit. It is more accurate to say it constrains the
*distribution* of appointments rather than the *standard* for them. Nothing
in it requires appointing an unqualified person. A state can set a
competency floor and then apply representational balance *above* that floor
— which is how the principle is defended by its authors.

**Where it genuinely bites:** where the qualified pool is unevenly
distributed, a floor plus a quota can become unfillable, and the usual
resolution is to lower the floor. That is a real problem and a design
problem, not a constitutional prohibition.

**Verdict: no amendment needed.** Chapter 2 is executable. Amend the chapter
to say floor-then-balance explicitly, rather than treating the principle as
an enemy.

### Chapter 3 — Security: BLOCKED
**Sections 214 and 215.** s.214(1) establishes the Nigeria Police Force and
provides that *no other police force shall be established for the Federation
or any part thereof*. s.215 places command under the Inspector-General and
constrains a governor's power to direct the Commissioner of Police in his
state.

This is the hardest constitutional block in the book, and it is why Chapter
3 proposes a community-intelligence layer rather than a police service.
Every state security outfit in Nigeria — Amotekun, CJTF, and the rest —
exists in the space s.214 leaves: gathering, patrolling, complementing, but
never policing.

**What a state can do without amendment:** fund, train, vet and statutorily
constitute an intelligence and patrol organ with a legal duty to route
information to federal forces; publish incident and complaints data.
**What it cannot do:** arrest, detain, prosecute, or command.

**The amendment route:** state police has been proposed repeatedly and has
never passed. Node 5's gate logic explains why — it moves both money and
control, and the gate-holders lose both. Expect the same trajectory as the
revenue formula, not the electricity amendment.

### Chapter 4 — Accountability: CLEAR
Procurement law, audit institutions and open-government commitments are all
state competences. Kaduna's 39/39 transparency score was achieved without
any constitutional change. The Fiscal Responsibility Act and state
equivalents provide the statutory hooks.

**Nothing here requires Abuja.** This is the cheapest and most legally
unobstructed chapter in the book — which makes its non-adoption purely a
political choice.

### Chapter 5 — The Constitution itself: THIS IS THE FIGHT
Not a chapter to execute but a campaign to run.

**The keystone:** s.162(2) requires the President to table RMAFC's
allocation proposals before the National Assembly, but sets **no time limit**
for doing so. The 2022 proposal died in that silence, as did four before it.
**The amendment is one clause: a transmission deadline.**

**Amendment mechanics (s.9):** an ordinary amendment requires two-thirds of
each chamber of the National Assembly *and* approval by the Houses of
Assembly of **not less than two-thirds of all states** — 24 of 36. Entrenched
provisions (including s.9 itself and Chapter IV rights) require four-fifths
of NASS and the same 24 states.

That arithmetic is the whole strategy: **24 state assemblies is the number.**
The LG-autonomy trajectory (0 states in 2015 → short by six in 2023) shows
the number is movable over roughly a decade.

### Chapter 6 — Identity: CLEAR, NEEDS COOPERATION
Nothing constitutional blocks a state from running contact programmes,
publishing posting-integrity data, or using Pidgin in official
communication.

**But NYSC is a federal scheme** established by its own Act, so the highest
-value intervention (protecting cross-ethnic posting integrity) requires
federal cooperation rather than state action. A state can host well, publish
its own hosting data, and run sub-graduate contact programmes alone.

### Chapter 7 — Education: CLEAR
Basic education is on the **Concurrent Legislative List**, and states and
local governments run it in practice. A state's discretionary share of
transfers to its local governments is set by state law. **The entire Ceará
mechanism is executable without touching the Constitution** — which is why
this chapter, despite being the most expensive, is also among the most
immediately available.

### Chapter 8 — Elections: FEDERAL, AND BLOCKED AT THE LG TIER
National elections are INEC's under the Constitution and the Electoral Act;
real-time upload is a legislative question already partly answered by the
2026 Act.

**The LG tier is the constitutional problem.** s.197 establishes State
Independent Electoral Commissions; s.198 and the Third Schedule govern
appointment, with members appointed by the Governor subject to state
assembly confirmation. That is the structure producing 375-of-376 results.

**Transferring LG elections to INEC requires a constitutional amendment**,
because SIECs are constitutionally established bodies with constitutionally
assigned functions. This is the one place in the book where our
recommendation is *centralisation*, and it is also constitutionally hard —
because it requires 24 state assemblies to vote against their own governors'
control of local elections.

**Honest assessment: this is the least likely amendment in the book to
pass**, and the chapter should say so rather than implying it is available.

### Land & housing: CLEAR — and the irony is the point
The **Land Use Act 1978** is incorporated into the Constitution (s.315(5)),
and it vests all land in each state in **the Governor**, held in trust.

So the person with total constitutional authority over land administration
is the same person complaining that titling is slow. **There is no gate here
except the governor himself.** A state that wanted to cut first-registration
fees to a token and run systematic ward-by-ward titling could do it by
executive action next quarter.

*Caveat, against our own enthusiasm:* the Act's vesting is also what makes
Nigerian land tenure insecure — a Certificate of Occupancy is a leasehold
from the governor, not freehold. Reformers have argued for decades that the
Act itself should be amended or repealed. Cutting fees is executable now;
converting occupancy into ownership is a constitutional question we have not
investigated and should not pretend to have answered.

### Oil & gas: BLOCKED
s.162(2)'s derivation proviso (not less than 13% of natural-resource
revenue) is constitutional. States cannot alter it. The Host Community
Development Trusts under the PIA are statutory, not constitutional, and are
therefore the only movable part.

## What this audit changes

**1. The units-first thesis is stronger than we stated it.** Four chapters
plus land administration are fully executable with no constitutional change:
revenue, accountability, education, identity (partly), land. Together they
cover most of the Blueprint's certified mechanisms. **A governor who did
nothing except these five could run most of this book.**

**2. The blocked list is short and specific.** Police (ss.214–215), LG
elections (ss.197–198), derivation (s.162(2)), and the transmission deadline
(s.162(2)). Four provisions. Not "the Constitution."

**3. We have been sloppy about federal character.** It constrains
distribution, not standard. Chapter 2 amended accordingly.

**4. The hardest amendment is the one we recommend most confidently.**
Moving LG elections to INEC requires 24 state assemblies to vote against
their own governors' control of local elections. Chapter 8 should state that
plainly rather than presenting centralisation as an available option.

**5. The Land Use Act is the sharpest irony in the corpus.** The governor
already holds total authority over the thing he says is stuck.

## Owed

- **Whether the Land Use Act should be amended** — we have investigated
  titling costs, not tenure security. Different question, not answered here.
- **Whether state police has ever come close to passing** and what the vote
  distribution was; would test whether the LG-autonomy trajectory model
  applies.
- **Judicial routes.** We know from Node 5 that courts declare and do not
  disburse. Whether any chapter has a viable litigation path is unexamined.
