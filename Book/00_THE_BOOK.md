# PART ONE — WHY NOTHING WORKS

## Chapter 1: Mama Ngozi pays four times

Mama Ngozi sells tomatoes in Ariaria market, Aba.

On Monday a man comes with a receipt book. Market levy. She pays. On
Wednesday another man comes, different book, different colour. Sanitation.
She pays. On Friday, two boys she has never seen collect what they call
"association fee." She pays that too, because the last woman who refused
found her stall locked.

Then at the end of the month, somebody from the local government comes for
the actual tax.

Four payments. One woman. One stall.

Now here is the part that should make you angry, and it is not the part
you think. **Three of those four payments never reach government at all.**
They go into somebody's pocket at the collection point. The government that
is supposed to build the road in front of Mama Ngozi's stall receives a
fraction of what Mama Ngozi actually paid.

So Mama Ngozi is right when she says government takes her money and does
nothing. And the government is right when it says it has no money. Both are
telling the truth. **The money left her hand and never arrived.**

That gap — between what citizens pay and what government receives — has a
name in this book. We call it **the leak**. Remember it. It will explain
more than you expect.

### What happened when one state closed the leak

Enugu State did something simple in 2023. They stopped collecting cash.

Not "reduced." Stopped. Every ministry, every agency, every office. If you
owe the state money, you pay into an account, and you get a receipt with a
number on it that can be checked. No man with a book. No boys at the stall.

You cannot steal what you cannot touch.

In one year, the money reaching Enugu State government went from ₦37.6
billion to ₦180.5 billion.

Read that again. **They did not raise anybody's tax.** The people of Enugu
were already paying. The money was simply arriving somewhere else. When the
state closed the leak, the money came home.

*[Where this comes from: National Bureau of Statistics figures for 2023 and
2024, confirmed by BudgIT, an independent Nigerian organisation that checks
government numbers. Both are public. Go and look.]*

### The honest part

We should tell you two things about Enugu, because this book has a rule: we
do not hide the parts that weaken our own argument.

**First**, prices in Nigeria roughly doubled over those same two years. So
that ₦180 billion is not worth what ₦180 billion was worth before. When we
did the sum properly, the real increase was about five times, not ten. Still
enormous. But we were quoting a bigger number than we should have, and we
corrected it ourselves before anybody caught us.

**Second**, Enugu borrowed heavily while doing this — its debt more than
doubled. And it has not published its audited accounts for 2024. We do not
know what those accounts would show. Neither does anybody else, and that
itself is a problem this book comes back to.

We could have left both of those out. Every politician would have. **We are
not asking you to trust us. We are asking you to check us — so we have to
show you the weak parts ourselves.**

## Chapter 2: The borehole that died

There is a borehole in your area, or your village, or the one you grew up
in. Somebody commissioned it. There were photographs. Maybe a ribbon.

It worked for a while. Then the pump failed, or the tap broke, or the panel
was stolen, and now it stands there like a monument to a day when somebody
came and made a speech.

You are not imagining a pattern. **Four out of every ten boreholes in
Nigeria stop working within two years.**

Four out of ten. Inside two years.

And the reason is stupid, which is what makes it painful. Nobody budgeted
to maintain it. The money to *build* it existed — there was a contract,
there was a commissioning. The money to *keep it working* was never in any
document at all.

### This is not only about water

Once you see it, you will see it everywhere.

A health centre gets renovated. Fresh paint, new sign. But there is no
electricity, so the fridge for vaccines does not run, and there is no water,
so nobody can deliver a baby safely there. On paper, that centre has been
"revitalised." In life, it is a painted building.

The people who count these things counted them. Nigeria promised to fix
17,600 health centres in four years — one for every ward in the country. By
the middle of 2025, about 1,295 were done. **That is 7.3% of the promise.**

Here is the surprising part, and it is the first piece of good news in this
book: **the government itself published the data that shows 7.3%.** They put
the numbers online, and a Nigerian organisation checked them and published
the embarrassing figure.

That is what it looks like when somebody is counting. Hold that thought.

### The pattern, named

Nigeria is very good at building things and very bad at keeping them.

We commission. We do not maintain. There is money in the budget for the
ribbon and no money in the budget for the pump that will fail in eighteen
months.

**Nigeria's problem is not building. It is keeping.**

*[Where this comes from: WASHNORM, the national water survey run with the
National Bureau of Statistics and UNICEF. The health figures are from the
National Primary Health Care Development Agency's own dashboard, which
anyone can open, and were computed by Nigeria Health Watch.]*

## Chapter 3: The teacher who could not pass the test

In 2017, Kaduna State gave a test to its primary school teachers.

The test was pitched at primary four level. The teachers were being tested
on what a ten-year-old should know.

Twenty-one thousand seven hundred and eighty failed.

Out of about thirty-three thousand.

Sit with that number. Two out of three teachers in the state's primary
schools could not pass a primary four paper. And those are the people who
were in front of your children every morning.

### What the governor did, and what happened after

He sacked them. All 21,780, and hired 25,000 new ones through a screening
process.

The union struck. The Nigeria Labour Congress shut the state down for a
week. They took him to court. He sacked the union's own national president
for refusing to sit the test. He won every single fight.

Then in 2023 his term ended and he left office.

**No test has been held since.**

The new government did not reverse the sackings — that would have been
painful. It simply stopped testing. It extended the retirement age instead,
raised allowances, released money to the union. The fight was over, and the
side that lost every battle won by waiting.

### The lesson, and it is the most important one in this book

The governor did not lose. **He was outlasted.**

And here is what made the difference, from the same state, in the same
handover. Kaduna also built a system for publishing government contracts —
what was awarded, to whom, for how much. That system did not die when the
governor left. It grew.

Why did one survive and the other die?

**Because the contracts system was written into law, and had people outside
government who would shout if it stopped.** The teacher test was one man's
practice. It lived in his will. When his will left the building, it left
too.

> **If it lives in one man, it dies with him.**
>
> **If it lives in law, and somebody outside government would shout, it
> survives.**

Write that on your hand. Everything in this book that works, works because
of it. Everything that failed, failed for want of it.

*[Where this comes from: the test and dismissals were reported across
Nigerian media at the time and confirmed by the state. The contracts system
is scored 39 out of 39 by BudgIT — the highest in Nigeria. Both are public.]*

## Chapter 4: Who is counting?

Now we can tell you the thing this book exists to say. It took us sixteen
separate investigations to see it.

We looked at sixteen parts of Nigerian government. Money. Police. Schools.
Elections. Water. Land. Roads. Health. And in each one we asked one small,
boring question:

**Who is counting?**

Not who is in charge. Not who is corrupt. Just: when this thing works or
fails, **who writes down the number, and do they work for the person being
measured?**

Five of the sixteen failed that question. And they were the same five
everybody complains about.

**Elections for local government.** The governor appoints the commission and
pays for it. In 2025: Lagos ruling party won 375 out of 376 seats. Osun
ruling party won 332 out of 332. Ondo ruling party won 203 out of 203.
Different states. Different parties. Same result.

**Land.** The state runs the land registry, sets the fees, processes the
applications and reports its own success. Nobody counts how many titles were
actually issued.

**Electricity.** In 2023 sixteen states took over regulating power in their
states. Three years later, nobody can tell you whether supply improved —
because the same states that took over the job also took over the counting.

And the eleven that passed? In every one of them, **somebody outside was
counting.** A federal agency. A civic organisation walking to project sites
with a camera. An international survey.

> **Where somebody outside is counting, things get better.**
> **Where nobody is counting, "we have fixed it" is a sentence nobody can
> check.**

### Why this explains Mama Ngozi, the borehole, and the teacher

Go back.

Mama Ngozi's money leaks because **nobody counts what she paid against what
arrived.**

The borehole dies because **nobody counts how many are still working** —
only how many were built.

The teacher test died because **nobody outside government was keeping score
of whether it continued**, so stopping it cost nothing.

One question. Three chapters. **Who is counting?**

## Chapter 5: So what is actually wrong with Nigeria?

Here is the answer, and it is not the one you expected.

Thirty of Nigeria's thirty-six states get between 71 and 92 out of every 100
naira they spend from oil money shared in Abuja.

Think about what that means for a governor. His money arrives whether or not
you work. Whether or not your children learn. Whether or not you are safe.
Whether or not a single business in his state succeeds.

**He does not need you.**

This is not because Nigerian leaders are worse people than other people. Put
a saint in that chair and the arrangement is the same. Nothing about it
makes serving you the way to keep power.

And then the second half, which is the part almost nobody says:

**Nobody outside is counting whether he did anything.**

A man who does not need you, and who nobody is checking. That is the whole
problem, in one sentence. Everything else — the leaking market levies, the
dead boreholes, the teachers who cannot read, the roads that were paid for
and never built — grows out of those two facts.

### What this means for you

It means the problem is **fixable**, and that is not optimism, it is
arithmetic.

You cannot fix "Nigerians are corrupt." That is not a problem, it is an
insult, and it has no next step.

You *can* fix "the state does not need its citizens" — you change what the
state lives on.

You *can* fix "nobody is counting" — you make somebody count.

Both have been done. Some of them have been done **inside Nigeria, by
Nigerians, in states you can name, in the last three years.** The rest of
this book is those cases.
