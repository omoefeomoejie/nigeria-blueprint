# What This Book Costs

*The red team's Tier 3.4 finding: not one chapter stated a price, and a
commissioner asks that first. This costs all eight.*

**What this is.** Order-of-magnitude estimates built from published Nigerian
figures where they exist, and marked as inference where they do not. It is
not a budget. Nobody should procure from it. It exists so that a reader can
tell which chapters are cheap, which are expensive, and — the more important
question — **which are expensive in money and which are expensive in
something else.**

For scale: that is **0.3% of Lagos's annual IGR**, **2% of Enugu's 2024
IGR**, and about **6% of Kaduna's**. It is less than a single flyover.

## Chapter by chapter

### Chapter 1 — Revenue: ₦250m–₦1bn
Anchored, not estimated. Lagos budgets **₦250m** for a digital tax
management system against a ₦200bn target; a companion data platform at
₦500m [Certified — Eko Revenue Plus documents]. Kwara bought POS machines,
a taxpayer e-Centre and payment integration with its land registry.
**Real constraint:** operational autonomy for the revenue service, and the
fight with people who live on cash collection.

### Chapter 2 — Merit selection: ₦150m–₦500m
**Cheaper than expected, because the infrastructure already exists.** States
are already running computer-based recruitment tests through **accredited
JAMB centres** — Adamawa used 13 of them statewide in 2025; Oyo and Ekiti
ran CBT exercises through university and polytechnic centres [Certified].
A state does not need to build testing capacity. It needs to rent it.

Cost is therefore: instrument design and independent examiner (the
load-bearing item), centre hire, invigilation, and publication. Recurring
cost is one test cycle a year.
**Real constraint:** not money. It is the organised loser who outwaits the
founder (Kaduna), and the independent examiner that makes the result
legitimate.

### Chapter 3 — Community intelligence: ₦300m–₦1bn capital, and salaries thereafter
**The only chapter whose recurring cost dominates.** Vetting, training,
equipment and the complaints mechanism are the capital items. But a
community intelligence service is people, and people are paid every month —
this is a permanent line, not a project.

Comparator: a police constable earns ₦54,000–62,000 monthly [Certified]. A
1,000-person service at that level is roughly **₦700m–₦750m a year in
salaries alone**, before equipment.
**Real constraint:** the constitutional ceiling, and abuse risk. Both are
recorded in the chapter.

### Chapter 4 — Accountability chain: ₦200m–₦600m
Portal and OCDS publication are software costs comparable to Chapter 1's.
The citizens' budget in Pidgin plus two languages and radio explainers is
cheap. The **participatory audit function is the recurring item**, and the
independent verification agent is the one that cannot be safely
under-funded [see `Research Engine/REAL_TERMS.md` and the Absorption
Playbook on donor-funded verifiers].
**Real constraint:** willingness to be embarrassed by your own data. Nigeria
already has an example — NPHCDA published a dashboard and a watchdog
immediately computed 7.3% from it.

### Chapter 5 — Constitutional route: near zero
Publishing a gate map, convening willing states, securing assembly
endorsements. **This chapter costs almost nothing and is the hardest in the
book**, which is the point: its constraint is entirely political. Legal
drafting and convening costs are rounding errors.

### Chapter 6 — Identity: ₦100m–₦400m
The posting-integrity audit is an analysis of existing NYSC records — a
research cost, not a programme cost, and nobody has ever published that
number. Extended-contact cohorts below graduate level are the programme
item, and Nigeria already runs national sports and skills competitions that
could carry the mixing requirement at marginal cost.
**Real constraint:** federal cooperation, since NYSC is not a state body.

### Chapter 7 — Learning-linked transfers: ₦500m–₦2bn, and this one is genuinely expensive
**The assessment is the cost, and it recurs annually.** A credible P2
literacy and P5 numeracy assessment across every LG in a state means
instrument design, enumerator training, fieldwork across hundreds of
schools, marking, and publication — every year, forever, or the mechanism
dies.

Anchoring is weak: Nigeria publishes no per-pupil assessment cost. Household
survey benchmarks exist but measure something else. **This is our least
confident number and it is marked as such.**

Two things reduce it. The **National Learning Assessment already exists**
federally [Certified — UBEC]; if it is credible and state-disaggregated, a
state's cost collapses to verification rather than administration. And the
transfer money itself is **not new spending** — it is a reallocation of
transfers a state already sends.
**Real constraint:** the assessment must never be run by the state whose
transfers depend on it.

### Chapter 8 — Elections: not a state cost
Real-time upload is federal, legislative, and already partly legislated. A
state's contribution is political pressure and civic mirroring of upload
rates, which civil society can do at negligible cost.

## What the costing actually shows

**1. Money is not the binding constraint in this book.** Seven of eight
chapters cost less than a single interchange. The four a governor can start
alone total roughly ₦1.1bn–₦4.1bn — about 0.3% of Lagos's IGR and 6% of
Kaduna's.

**2. The expensive things are recurring, not capital.** Chapter 3's salaries
and Chapter 7's annual assessment are the only items that get harder each
year rather than easier. Everything else is a one-off purchase followed by
maintenance.

**3. Which maps exactly onto Rule 7.** Capital costs buy **stock**;
recurring costs buy **flow**. A state that funds the platform and not the
audit, or the training and not the salaries, has bought the half that
persists by default and skipped the half that dies at succession. **The
costing and the irreversibility test point at the same line item.**

**4. The cheapest chapter is the hardest.** Chapter 5 costs almost nothing
and has failed five times since 2001. Anyone who thinks Nigeria's problem is
money should read that row twice.

## Owed

- Every figure here except Chapter 1's is **inference from comparators**,
  not a quoted price. Marked throughout.
- **No state publishes what its reforms cost.** That absence is itself a
  finding: it is impossible to compare the price of governance across
  Nigerian states, which is another missing scorekeeper.
- Chapter 7's assessment cost is the weakest number in this file and the
  most consequential, because it recurs.
