# Power

*Nigeria opened a constitutional door in 2023. Sixteen states walked
through it. Two years later the lights are not noticeably better.*

*This is the sector where our own advice was tested and came back wrong.*

---

## 1. What the sector produces now

Electricity left the exclusive federal list by constitutional amendment in
March 2023; the Electricity Act followed in June [Certified, Node 5].

Since then NERC has transferred regulatory oversight to **16 states**, on a
published sequence running from Enugu, Ekiti and Ondo (October 2024) through
Imo, Oyo, Edo, Kogi, Lagos, Ogun, Niger, Plateau and Abia, to Anambra,
Nasarawa and Bayelsa in early 2026 [Certified — NERC]. Any state that passes
its own electricity law, establishes a regulator and notifies NERC receives
an order transferring oversight of everything generated, distributed and
consumed inside its borders.

That is the fastest devolution of a federal competence in Nigeria's
post-1999 history.

**And:** two years in, independent assessment finds efforts still focused on
institutional setup and restructuring, with metering gaps and weak network
infrastructure unresolved and **little meaningful improvement in actual power
supply so far** [Certified].

## 2. What is broken

**The power was transferred; the capacity was not.** Reporting on 16 states
finds several struggling to build working systems — some without fully
operational State Electricity Regulatory Commissions, and in some cases
licences issued outside the legal regulatory framework [Certified].

**Regulatory improvisation.** Imo had not formally licensed the existing
distributor serving the state, yet within weeks of its regulator's
establishment issued a licence to a new entity to generate, transmit and
distribute — without a clearly defined process, raising questions about
market coordination and jurisdiction [Certified]. That is not decentralised
regulation. It is a vacuum with letterhead.

**The physical problem was never regulatory.** Metering gaps and weak
networks do not care which commission licenses them. Transferring oversight
changes who signs, not what is built.

**Money.** India unbundled in 2003 and its lesson is blunt: without
cost-reflective tariffs, electricity markets cannot survive financially
[Certified]. Nigerian states now own that problem too.

## 3. Which horizontal failure explains each part

| Broken part | Horizontal failure | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Regulators exist on paper, not in function | Low state capacity — the first principle itself | Part II, Layer 4 |
| Licences outside the framework | No accountability machinery around a new power | Ch. 4 |
| Nothing measured; "supply improved" is unverifiable | Measurement absent by default | Ch. 4, Rule 8 |
| Reform stalls between announcement and delivery | Stock acquired, flow never built | Rule 7 |

**And the correction to our own work.** Chapter 5 said: take every
burden-shedding devolution now, because each grows unit capacity. Power
shows that acquiring a power is not the same as acquiring the ability to use
it. Sixteen states took a door and, in several cases, produced a *worse*
regulatory environment than the federal monopoly they replaced. See
Mistakes 8.1. The corrected prescription is **take the open door and build
the regulator first** — devolution is a capacity project wearing a legal
costume.

## 4. What is genuinely sector-specific

**Integrated private supply is the only thing visibly working.** Aba's
ring-fenced, relatively stable system is driven by private-sector
integrated generation and distribution (Geometric Power / Aba Power), built
over two decades [Certified]. The success case is not a state regulator; it
is a utility that owns generation and distribution together in one
industrial city.

**Tariff reality is unavoidable.** Unlike education or agriculture, this
sector has an arithmetic floor: electricity that is sold below cost cannot
be supplied indefinitely, whoever regulates it. India's twenty-year
experience is the warning [Certified].

**The physical asset base is the binding constraint.** Meters, lines,
transformers. No regulatory arrangement substitutes for them.

## 5. What one state could try in 18 months

Powers required: already held by any of the 16 transferred states.

1. **Build the regulator before using the power.** Staffed, funded,
   published rules, a licensing process written down before the first
   licence. States that skipped this produced the Imo problem.
2. **Publish a supply baseline** — hours of supply by feeder, metering rate,
   collection rate — before claiming anything. Nobody can currently verify
   "improvement" in any of the 16 states, which is why the question is being
   answered by press release.
3. **Ring-fence one industrial cluster** on the Aba pattern: integrated
   generation plus distribution, private capital, cost-reflective tariff,
   measured. Prove the model in one place rather than promising a state.
4. **Meter first, then tariff.** Unmetered customers cannot be billed
   cost-reflectively, and un-cost-reflective markets die (India).

**Kill condition.** If after 18 months the regulator has issued licences but
published no supply baseline, the state has acquired a power and used it to
create paperwork. Stop licensing; build the measurement.

### Scorekeeper note — a sharper version of this brief's finding

This brief says the 2023 devolution produced "little meaningful improvement
in supply." The scorekeeper test gives a more precise and more damning
statement: **the devolution produced no measurable anything, because it
moved the scorer along with the authority.**

Before 2023, NERC regulated and reported on a national market. After, each
state regulator licenses the operators in its own market and reports on the
market it created. There is no independent body computing supply hours,
metering rates or collection rates state by state. So "several states are
struggling" is itself an inference from licensing irregularities rather than
from performance data, because performance data does not exist.

**Design consequence for §5:** the published supply baseline must be
computed to a national standard that NERC still maintains, so that sixteen
state markets remain comparable. A baseline each state defines for itself is
not a baseline; it is a press release with a number in it.
