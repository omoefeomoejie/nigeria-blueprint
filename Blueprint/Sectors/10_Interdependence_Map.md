# What Has to Move First

*A governor cannot fix eight sectors at once. This asks a narrower question:
if you can only start one, which one unlocks the others?*

---

## Why this exists

Sector Brief 03 found that a "functional" primary health centre requires
reliable electricity and water. So health's ceiling is set by power's floor
and water's floor. Once you see that, you cannot un-see it: these sectors
are not eight separate problems, they are a dependency graph, and the order
you attack them in changes what you get.

**Status warning.** The individual dependencies below are documented in the
briefs. The *map* — the ranking that follows from them — is inference. It is
marked as such and should be attacked accordingly.

## The dependencies, as documented

| This sector | Depends on | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Primary health | **Power, water** | A functional Level 2 PHC requires 24-hour service, reliable electricity, water supply [Sector 03, NPHCDA definition] |
| Basic education | **Power** | Smart schools, digital learning, solar-powered facilities are the stated intervention model [Sector 04] |
| Water | **Power** | Solar-powered schemes are standard for new water points; pumping needs energy [Sector 05, REA/World Bank] |
| Agriculture | **Transport** | Input delivery and market access; extension requires reach [Sector 01] |
| Revenue (property) | **Land registry** | Property tax needs a register; 90%+ untitled [Sector 07, Chapter 1] |
| Everything | **Absorption** | Authority, money and infrastructure all go unused where the state cannot operate them [Part II, Layer 3] |
| Everything | **Selection** | Every sector is run by people someone chose [Chapter 2] |
| Selection | **Elections** | Who chooses the choosers [Chapter 8] |

## What the map shows

**Power is the deepest physical dependency.** It sits under health, education
and water. Nothing else in the physical sectors has three dependents. A state
that fixes power raises three other ceilings without touching them.

**But power is also the sector where the state most recently failed.**
Sixteen states took electricity oversight and could not operate it
[Sector 02]. So the deepest dependency is also the hardest lift, which is
exactly the kind of trap that makes governments start with the easy thing
and wonder why nothing moves.

**Absorption sits under everything, including power.** This is the finding
that reorders the map. It is not a sector; it is the state's ability to use
what it holds. Fix it and every other sector's investment starts converting.
Ignore it and every other sector's investment joins ₦332bn of idle education
money and 40% of boreholes dead in two years.

**Land registry is the cheapest high-leverage move.** It requires no
construction, no grid, no new institution — only a decision to stop charging
15–30% of a property's value to write down who owns it [Sector 07]. And it
feeds Chapter 1's revenue layer directly, which funds everything else.

## The sequence this implies

**Zero. Absorption first, and it is not a sector.**
Pay on verified output. Publish a completion rate. No new award while an old
one is abandoned. This costs nothing, requires no capital, and determines
whether anything below produces results [Absorption Playbook].

**One. Land registry and revenue integrity, together.**
Cheapest, fastest, no construction, and they fund the rest. Cut first-
registration fees, kill cash collection, publish the conversion ledger
[Chapter 1, Sector 07].

**Two. Power, in one ring-fenced place.**
Not statewide. One industrial cluster on the Aba pattern, with the regulator
built before the licences are issued [Sector 02]. It raises three ceilings,
but only where it actually works, so prove it small.

**Three. Water billing and maintenance, then health functionality.**
Water before health, because a PHC without water is a building. Fix billing
first so maintenance is funded, then audit health facilities against the
functional definition rather than the refurbishment count [Sectors 05, 03].

**Four. Education incentives, once measurement exists.**
The Ceará mechanism needs a baseline and an absorption fix at the front
[Chapter 7, Sector 04]. It is not first because it cannot be — the
assessment has to exist and the LGs have to be able to receive money.

**Running throughout: selection.**
Every sector above is run by people. Selection reform has no natural
position in the sequence because it is a condition of all of them
[Chapter 2].

## What this map warns against

**Starting with the visible.** Roads and buildings photograph well and sit
high in the graph, not deep. They depend on absorption and revenue and
support little else. A state that starts here gets ribbon-cuttings and
₦5.71tn of rollovers.

**Starting with the biggest number.** Oil and gas is the largest sector by
value and the smallest by state-level actionability, with the worst delivery
record in the federation [Sector 09]. Size is not leverage.

**Treating sectors as independent.** Every ministry writes a plan as if its
sector were alone. Health cannot deliver past power's ceiling regardless of
its budget, and no health budget fixes that.

## Owed

- **The map is inference.** Each dependency is documented; the ranking is
  not. A state that runs this sequence and measures the result would settle
  it, and nobody has.
- **The reverse dependencies are unmapped.** Does health failure hold back
  education? Does insecurity hold back agriculture? Both are plausible and
  neither was investigated.
- **Trade and investment (Brief 08)** is missing from the graph.
